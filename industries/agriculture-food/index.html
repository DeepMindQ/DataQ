Agriculture & Food Systems

A bushel of corn gets weighed at the elevator, settled against a contract, hedged by a merchandiser, and consumed months later as an ingredient nobody can trace back to a field without three phone calls. Every step already produces data — FMIS records on the grower side, scale tickets and grain accounting at the elevator, run orders and lot genealogy in the packhouse, pallets and temperature logs in distribution. Dataq Software builds the cross-chain data fabric and the decision services that run on it, so the chain reads like one ledger instead of five separate ones.

Agriculture and food is not one industry; it is a relay of very different businesses, each digitized separately by vendors who never talk to each other. Growers run FMIS platforms and machinery telematics. Elevators run scale systems and grain accounting. Merchandisers run commodity CTRM/ETRM desks. Packhouses run run orders, MES events, and quality lab records. Distributors run WMS, TMS, and reefer telemetry. Lenders run loan files against warehouse receipts and APH histories. Anyone who has tried to answer a simple question across the chain — what tons of a specific lot remain in a specific bin, committed to which contract, with what grade and shrink history, and what pallets descended from it — knows the answer currently requires exports, spreadsheets, and a week of reconciliation.

The seams show up as money. Shrink at the elevator that nobody can decompose into moisture removal, handling, and settle-out. Basis and hedge-to-arrive positions that drift from the physical tons they were supposed to cover. A recall notice that becomes a two-week document hunt because lot genealogy stops at the plant gate. Operating lines that cannot take warehouse receipts as collateral because nobody trusts the inventory numbers enough to lend against them. Premium and sustainability programs that pay on honor-system questionnaires because practice evidence could not be assembled in time. None of these are abstract analytics problems; they are data plumbing and decision-logic problems with a price tag.

Dataq Software works the way a good integrator works inside a plant: read-first on systems of record, build the connective fabric on top, and put decision services where the operators already sit. We do not replace the FMIS, the grain accounting suite, the CTRM, or the WMS — we make them legible to each other and automate the decisions that span them. Where a link in the chain is not worth connecting, we say so and stop. The usual starting point is a two-week baseline: settlement statements, traceability records, and position reports read against your own data, with the gap quantified before anyone commits to a build.

Where the chain breaks: five seams and the estates on either side

Each segment's data lives in a different estate, bought in a different decade, with different identifiers for what is physically the same thing. A field is an ID in the FMIS, a parcel in the lender's file, and a delivery route in the hauler's dispatch. A lot is a traceability lot code in the plant, a production run in the MES, and a pallet license plate in the WMS. Grain is a scale ticket at the elevator, a position in the CTRM, and a warehouse receipt at the bank. Nothing is wrong with any of those systems alone; the chain breaks because nobody maintains the join between them.

  • Grower to elevator

    Harvest logs and FMIS records rarely survive the truck ride. Scale tickets carry different field names, moisture and dockage get applied inconsistently, and origin claims — field, variety, program enrollment — are re-typed by hand or lost.

  • Elevator to merchandising

    Grain accounting and the CTRM run on separate ledgers. Contract fill against scale tickets reconciles monthly at best, so hedge-to-arrive and basis positions drift from the physical tons they cover.

  • Plant to distribution

    Lot genealogy usually stops at the plant gate. The WMS knows pallets; the MES knows production runs; nobody maintains the join between them, which is exactly the join a recall depends on.

  • Distribution to retail

    FEFO discipline depends on case-level date codes, but allocation and markdown decisions run on DC-level aggregates, so aging inventory is discovered at the shelf instead of in the plan.

  • Chain to capital

    Lenders and insurers price risk off APH yields, acreage reports, and inventory in yet another estate nobody reconciles against operations — which is why working capital against inventory stays a slide deck instead of a program.

One pattern repeats at every seam: the physical flow was engineered, while the data flow was improvised. Fixing the seams is less about new systems than about a maintained join — identifiers, timestamps, and quantities carried across boundaries by software instead of by re-typing.

Grower-side data: FMIS, machinery logs, and what the harvest actually recorded

The grower estate is richer than most chain participants assume. A farm management information system (FMIS) holds field boundaries, practice records, as-applied maps, and input purchases. Machinery writes ISOBUS task data and streams telematics to OEM clouds. Yield monitors write harvest logs — noisy, but real. Soil labs return grids and zones. None of it is organized for chain use: the elevator needs origin and tons, the insurer needs APH-quality yields, the sustainability program needs practice evidence, and the FMIS was built for the agronomist rather than for any of them. Spreadsheet exports fill the gap every harvest and dissolve by spring.

What we read first, and why read-only

Dataq Software starts by reading, not writing: field boundaries to build the master, harvest logs to establish per-field yields, as-applied records to document practice, and machinery data normalized through the ADAPT framework so John Deere, Case and New Holland, AGCO, and Trimble files resolve into one shape. From that read-only base, chain services become possible — origin claims attached to deliveries, satellite acreage verification against reported planted acres, and variable-rate prescriptions flowing back through the same pipe. If a grower's logs are too sparse to support a claim, we say so; evidence quality is measured, not assumed.

The payoff is not another dashboard. It is that a load can carry its origin with it: field boundary, variety, program enrollment, and practice history attached to the scale ticket instead of reconstructed after the fact by an insurance claim or a sustainability survey. That single join upgrades everything downstream. Premium audits stop being honor-system questionnaires; scope 3 land-use accounting starts from practice records instead of purchase-led estimates; insurance documentation reconciles against measured yields; and recall traceability inherits origin instead of rediscovering it. The FMIS keeps serving the agronomist — it simply stops being a dead end for the chain.

  • Field boundary master

    Stable field identities with versioned geometry and tenure, built once from FMIS and county records, so every measurement the chain takes joins to the same ground.

  • Harvest log normalization

    Yield monitor logs normalized through ADAPT and cleaned enough for origin claims and APH documentation — measured evidence quality, not assumptions.

  • As-applied practice records

    Planting, spraying, and application events from machines and FMIS, keyed to boundaries, forming the evidence base programs and audits consume.

  • Origin claims at intake

    Field, variety, and program enrollment attached to deliveries at the scale or intake point, so premiums and traceability inherit origin instead of rediscovering it.

Grain origination: scale tickets, shrink, and settlement arithmetic

An elevator is a machine for converting truckloads into positions, and its truth lives in the scale ticket and the settlement statement. Grade, moisture, dockage, and test weight drive discounts; shrink drives the difference between gross and settle tons; storage, delayed-price, and hedge-to-arrive contracts drive who owns what between harvest and shipment. Grain accounting does this arithmetic well for a single location. What it does not do is answer chain-level questions: which contract positions are filled across all locations, what shrink is doing by commodity, season, and handling point, and whether settlement quality is drifting.

Shrink is the classic invisible loss. It hides in moisture adjustment methods, in settle-out between scale and bin, in handling losses, in unload cycles, and in the gap between ticket tons and storage tons. Operators know shrink exists; almost nobody can decompose it, because the inputs live in different logs — the scale system, bin levels, dryer runtimes, outbound shipments. We build the reconciliation so shrink becomes a measured, attributed number per location and season, and settlements get audited automatically: every settlement statement re-derived from tickets and contract terms, with exceptions flagged before money moves.

Storage contracts, delayed price, and warehouse receipts

Between harvest and shipment, ownership and risk live in contract types: storage agreements with fee schedules, delayed-price balances with deadline risk, warehouse receipts that make grain financeable, and hedge-to-arrive commitments that fix price ahead of delivery. Each type changes who bears what, and each generates records the reconciliation must respect — fee accruals, deadline aging, receipt issuance against stored tons. When those records stay inside the elevator, the bank and the grower both take someone's word for them. When they join the fabric, receipts and balances become verifiable positions, and the deadline that will hurt somebody surfaces in time to act.

Merchandising and risk: basis, hedge-to-arrive, and mark-to-market discipline

Merchandising runs on a commodity CTRM/ETRM: futures positions, basis positions, hedge-to-arrive contracts with growers, deferred-pricing balances, to-arrive purchases, and the mark-to-market that tells leadership what the book is worth today. The system of record exists and works. The failure mode is separation between the CTRM and physical grain accounting — positions filled on paper, coverage reconciled in spreadsheets, basis exposure computed against tons that may have already moved. When the two ledgers disagree, the discrepancy surfaces as margin variance or a surprise delivery obligation, never as a data quality report.

We join the two ledgers and make coverage a daily fact. Every hedge-to-arrive and delayed-price contract maps to scale tickets and settlement records; every futures position maps to the physical it covers or hedges; mark-to-market computes against realized settlements instead of assumptions. Merchandisers work from exception lists — uncovered hedge-to-arrive bushels, expired storage, positions filled twice — instead of month-end archaeology. Finance gets a mark-to-market trail the auditors can re-derive. Nothing in the CTRM is replaced; the decision layer reads it and closes the loop back to grain accounting, where the physical truth lives.

  • Contract coverage reconciliation

    Every hedge-to-arrive, delayed-price, and storage contract tied to tickets and settlement statements daily. Overfills, underfills, and expired commitments surface the day they happen, not at month end.

  • Basis and position reporting

    Basis exposure computed from actual open physical against open futures, by location and commodity, with mark-to-market traceable to settlement records an auditor can re-derive.

  • Margin and credit signals

    When a grower's hedge-to-arrive position moves against them, the credit file should know before the margin call does. Coverage data feeds the lending estate instead of staying inside the elevator.

  • Origination economics

    Netback and origin margin per bushel, per location, per contract type, so leadership knows which origination programs earn their premium and which only look busy.

Packhouse and processing: run orders, lot genealogy, and the yield ledger

A packhouse runs on run orders: what to pack, from which lots, for which customer, on which line, tonight. Around that sits a yield ledger nobody fully trusts — fruit in by lot, packs out by SKU, waste and rework somewhere in between, cull rates varying by variety, grower, and season. The MES or packhouse system records events; the genealogy between incoming lots, production runs, rework loops, and outbound pallets usually lives in someone's head or on paper travel cards. For most commodities, that genealogy is exactly what the FSMA 204 traceability rule now expects a firm to produce on demand.

We make lot genealogy a maintained join instead of a reconstruction project: incoming lot IDs from intake, production run records from the line, rework and co-pack loops, and outbound traceability lot codes on pallets and cases — one graph, queryable in minutes. The same graph powers the yield ledger: pack-out percentage by lot, variety, and line; waste attributed to the step that caused it; and run order planning fed by real conversion rates instead of last year's averages. If the plant's event capture is too sparse to support the genealogy, the baseline says so plainly, and the fix becomes instrumentation rather than software theater.

Allocation: where packhouses quietly lose margin

Allocation is the quiet margin leak: which customer's order gets the day's best-conditioned fruit, which lot goes to the program paying the premium, which run sequence minimizes changeover and waste. Those decisions get made on whiteboards because the inputs — intake condition, order book, line rates, program commitments — never sit in one place. The decision service does not replace the packing manager's judgment; it hands that judgment numbers in the order the shift actually thinks: tonight's run orders with expected pack-out, program coverage, and the lots that will miss a customer's spec if they wait another day.

  • Run order sequencing

    Tonight's run orders planned against line changeover costs, lot condition, and program commitments, with expected pack-out stated per order before the shift starts.

  • Pack-out and waste attribution

    Pack-out percentage by lot, variety, and line, with waste attributed to the step that caused it instead of absorbed as shrink.

  • Program segregation

    Identity-preserved and premium lots tracked separately through the genealogy graph, so segregation claims survive customer and regulator scrutiny.

Traceability as evidence: FSMA 204, EPCIS 2.0, and the recall clock

The FSMA 204 traceability rule turned traceability from a customer courtesy into a regulatory deliverable. Firms handling foods on the Food Traceability List must capture critical tracking events (CTEs) and key data elements (KDEs), assign traceability lot codes, maintain the one-step-back and one-step-forward links, and produce records — including the Food Traceability Supplement — promptly when FDA asks. Most food companies already hold the events somewhere: receiving records at intake, transformation records in the plant, shipping records in the WMS. What they cannot do is assemble those events into a chain in hours, because the records live in five systems with five identifier schemes.

We build the evidence chain on GS1 EPCIS 2.0 — commissioning, aggregation, transformation, and decommissioning events with the core business vocabulary — so the traceability answer becomes a query rather than an investigation. Mock recalls stop being theater: run against real data, they report the actual time to trace a lot one step back and one step forward, the percentage of events complete, and the KDEs missing at each CTE. Where a plant's capture is too thin — receiving without lot IDs, transformations without records — the report says so and scopes the instrumentation needed, honestly and specifically.

One step back: supplier records that arrive broken

The one-step-back half of traceability depends on suppliers whose records arrive as EDI fragments, spreadsheet attachments, and paper certificates, each with its own lot identifiers and date conventions. The normalization layer takes those inputs as they come, maps them into the same EPCIS event model the plant uses, and scores the result for completeness, so upstream gaps are visible before they become recall exposure. Where a supplier's records are permanently unusable, the scorecard says so, and procurement has something concrete to negotiate with instead of a quality anecdote.

Traceability quality is a lagging indicator of data discipline everywhere else. When scale tickets, lot codes, and shipping events share identifiers, the recall clock runs in minutes; when they do not, no amount of mock-recall rehearsal will conjure the joins under regulatory pressure.

  • CTE and KDE gap assessment

    Every required critical tracking event mapped to the system that holds it, and every key data element scored for capture quality — the difference between a compliant chain and a PDF collection project.

  • EPCIS 2.0 event repository

    Transformation, commissioning, and shipping events modeled once, written automatically from plant and WMS systems, queryable one-step-back and one-step-forward across the chain.

  • Mock recall drills on real data

    Time-to-trace measured in minutes, completeness in percent, and gaps reported by CTE — a number the QA team can take into an audit without embarrassment.

  • Supplier intake normalization

    One-step-back records arriving as EDI, spreadsheets, and paper, normalized into the same event model so upstream gaps surface before they matter.

Cold chain integrity: temperature excursions and the evidence trail

Perishables distribution is a race between remaining shelf life and remaining distance, and the telemetry already exists: reefer units log setpoints and return-air temperatures, DC cold rooms log temperature and door events, data loggers ride pallets, and the WMS knows every pallet's date code. What is missing is the join between temperature history and inventory decisions. A temperature excursion is currently an email chain. Quarantine happens late or not at all, affected pallets are guessed at, and a claim gets settled on negotiation strength because no party can prove the chain of custody.

The decision layer changes the defaults. Excursion detection evaluates temperature history against product tolerance bands rather than a fixed alarm threshold, and identifies affected pallets by lot and location within minutes, with the temperature evidence attached to the pallet record for claims and disposition. Release and quarantine become auditable decisions instead of phone calls, carrier scorecards start reflecting excursion frequency by lane, and the same evidence feeds customer claims in both directions — provable losses and defensible refusals.

Perishables S&OP: FEFO, markdown cadence, and retail replenishment

FEFO fails quietly in most chains: the policy exists, the warehouse tries to honor it, but allocation logic never sees the date codes at case level and nobody measures how often older stock shipped to longer lanes. Enforced as allocation logic, FEFO becomes a default that ships the oldest code first unless someone consciously overrides with a reason code — and the overrides, counted and read monthly, are where the real planning insight lives: which lanes, which customers, which categories chronically break date discipline.

A perishables S&OP built on remaining-shelf-life distributions plans differently from one built on average case life. Supply commitments, promotion timing, and markdown cadence align to what is actually aging in the network; DC-to-store allocation weighs transit time against code life; and replenishment inherits the same facts, so DCs stop pushing pallets the stores will have to discount anyway. Shrink stops being a single undifferentiated number and becomes an attributed one: markdown-driven, spoilage-driven, and logistics-driven, each with its own owner and its own fix.

Audit evidence: HACCP, GFSI schemes, and the paperwork nobody can find

Every certified food plant lives under HACCP plans and a GFSI-benchmarked scheme — BRCGS, SQF, FSSC 22000 — and every audit tests whether records can be produced: CCP monitoring logs, corrective actions, verification activities, sanitation records, calibration, supplier approvals, mock recall results. The procedures exist and are usually followed. The failure is retrieval: evidence scattered across clipboard scans, quality spreadsheets, and a document system that stores but does not connect. Audit preparation becomes a two-week scramble, and findings cluster around missing or incomplete records rather than unsafe process.

We treat audit evidence as a data product. The events that generate evidence already happen in the plant; the work is making their capture structured and their retrieval instant. Any CCP, batch, lot, or date range assembles into an audit packet in minutes, with completeness scoring that flags thin records before the auditor does. Corrective action closure rates, supplier approval currency, and verification schedules become standing metrics instead of pre-audit fire drills. The quality team keeps its procedures and its HACCP plan unchanged; what changes is that the evidence no longer depends on who happens to be on vacation.

Mock recalls as standing metrics, not annual theater

Most plants run one mock recall a year, timed for the audit, scored by a consultant, and forgotten. Run monthly against real data, the same exercise behaves differently: time-to-trace and completeness measured by CTE, trended by product family and shift, with the capture gaps it exposes fixed within the quarter rather than the week before the auditor arrives. The number the auditor sees is the number the plant has watched all year, which changes the conversation from documentation panic to demonstrated capability.

Lending and insurance: APH yields, acreage verification, and the credit file

Ag lending and crop insurance price risk off records the rest of the chain produces but never reconciles: actual production history (APH) yields, planted and insured acreage, inventory positions, warehouse receipts as collateral, and contract commitments. The lender's estate — loan origination, covenant tracking, borrowing base certificates — is a separate world from the elevator's grain accounting and the grower's FMIS. So operating lines get priced on stale balance sheets, receipts get financed without independent verification, insurance documentation becomes a springtime paper chase between grower, agent, and adjuster, and the same operation tells three different stories to three counterparties.

The connective work is straightforward to describe and rare in practice. Satellite acreage verification cross-checks reported planted acres before the line is drawn. APH yields compute from cleaned harvest records rather than handwritten histories. Borrowing bases refresh from actual grain accounting and warehouse receipt positions instead of quarterly attestations. Hedge-to-arrive exposure becomes visible to the credit officer the day the contract is signed, not when the margin call arrives. Where a grower's records are too thin to support verification, the honest answer is a smaller line, not a modeled one — a lender who cannot trust the number eventually stops asking for it.

  • Borrowing base verification

    Inventory-backed lines refreshed from grain accounting and warehouse receipt positions with audit trails, so the borrowing base reflects physical truth instead of quarterly attestations.

  • APH-grade yield documentation

    Cleaned harvest records assembled to actual production history standards, defensible to adjusters, and joined to the fields and practices that produced them.

  • Contract exposure in the credit file

    Hedge-to-arrive and deferred-price exposure visible to credit officers the day contracts are signed, priced into lines before margin calls make it urgent.

Sustainability programs: MRV, scope 3 land-use, and regenerative measurement

Regenerative agriculture programs and buyer scope 3 land-use accounting collide with the same problem: measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) requires evidence that practice actually changed — cover crops established, residue managed, fertilizer rates reduced, land retired — and the evidence must survive audit by the program, the buyer, and eventually the regulator. Most programs run on grower attestation plus spot checks. That is not MRV; it is a questionnaire with a payment attached, and both buyers and verifiers have started treating it accordingly.

The evidence pipeline assembles what already exists: practice records from the FMIS, as-applied data from machinery, planting and tillage signals from satellite imagery time series, yield outcomes from harvest logs — organized per field and per season into an MRV packet a verifier can sample. Scope 3 land-use reporting inherits the same structure instead of restarting from spend-based estimates. Regenerative program payments tie to verified practice rather than intention. When imagery or records cannot support a claim for a given field, that field is excluded from the program that year; credibility is worth more than enrollment.

  • Practice verification from imagery

    Cover crop establishment, residue cover, and tillage events detected from Sentinel-2 and drone imagery time series against reported practice, with disagreement flagged for follow-up before payment.

  • MRV evidence packets

    Per-field, per-season bundles — practice records, as-applied maps, imagery extracts, yields — assembled to the program's protocol so verification takes days, not seasons.

  • Scope 3 land-use accounting

    Supplier-level land-use change and field practice data rolled into buyer reporting at the granularity procurement needs, replacing spend-based estimates.

  • Enrollment honesty

    Fields that cannot evidence the claimed practice are excluded for that program year, because credibility with verifiers and buyers compounds the same way trial evidence does.

Fair price and premium programs: audit-grade measurement

Fair price programs and origin premiums — coffee, cocoa, cotton, specialty grains, identity-preserved commodities — promise growers a premium for practices or quality, then struggle to prove either. The premium is real; the audit trail is not. Payment reconciliations run on delivery tickets that may or may not carry origin, on practice attestations collected on paper, and on cooperative ledgers that blend lots the program was supposed to keep separate. When a buyer asks for assurance that premium dollars reached the growers who earned them, the honest answer is usually a sampling exercise, not a ledger.

Audit-grade measurement makes the premium traceable: origin claims attached to deliveries at the scale or intake, lots kept separate through the chain by the same genealogy a recall uses, practice documentation verified from imagery and records rather than attested, and per-grower premium statements derived from the ledger instead of reconstructed. The originator can then answer the buyer's question with data — which tons carried the premium, which growers were paid, what evidence supports each link. Programs that cannot produce this evidence are paying for a story, and their auditors increasingly know the difference.

Questions agriculture and food leaders ask us

Leaders ask versions of the same questions before anyone gets near their systems. These are the answers we give in the first conversation, before a statement of work exists, because they decide whether there is a project at all. If a question is missing here, the two-week baseline usually finds it. If an answer cannot be backed by your own data during the baseline, we treat that as a finding, not a talking point.

  • Can you do this without replacing our grain accounting system or WMS?

    Replacement is explicitly not the model. The fabric reads your systems of record and adds the joins and decision services they cannot provide across segments. If one system cannot support a required read, we scope that one interface, not a platform swap.

  • How fast can a real recall trace run?

    After the EPCIS event chain is built from your plant and WMS systems, one-step-back and one-step-forward traces assemble in minutes, with completeness reported per event. The baseline measures your current time first, so the improvement is a number, not a claim.

  • Is FSMA 204 compliance just a software purchase?

    No. The rule asks for capture and production of CTEs and KDEs; most of those records already exist in your estate. The work is normalization, joins, and retrieval speed, plus honest remediation where capture is genuinely missing.

  • What is shrink actually costing us?

    The baseline decomposes it as far as your logs allow: moisture adjustment method, settle-out, handling, and settlement errors separated and priced. Operators are often surprised by the settlement-error share; sometimes the finding is that shrink is already fine, and we say that too.

  • Can working capital actually be borrowed against inventory?

    Only against inventory a lender can verify. When borrowing bases refresh from grain accounting and warehouse receipt positions with audit trails, lenders price accordingly. We build the verification layer; your lenders decide what it is worth.

  • Who owns the grower data in these programs?

    The grower does, contractually and technically. We follow Ag Data Transparent principles: consent recorded per use, aggregation rules enforced in the fabric, and cooperative data structures supported rather than bypassed.

  • What if our data quality is bad?

    That is the normal starting condition, not a disqualification. The baseline measures which records are trustworthy enough for which decisions, and the roadmap fixes capture where it blocks a decision that matters — not a two-year data perfection program that blocks everything.
