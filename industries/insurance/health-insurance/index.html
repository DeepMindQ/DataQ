Health Insurance & Benefits

In health insurance, the product is a promise to pay for care — and every part of that promise runs through administrative machinery that decides faster than most carriers staff for. Prior authorization requests wait on clinical evidence buried in chart notes. Claims adjudicate on coded data that may not match the clinical narrative underneath. Risk adjustment revenue depends on conditions captured accurately or missed quietly. Quality measures (HEDIS) turn on gaps nobody closes until the measurement year ends. And every one of those decisions happens under HIPAA, CMS program audit and state DOI scrutiny. This page is the engineering picture of the payer machine as Dataq Software sees it: what runs, where it strains, and the six deep builds that move auto-adjudication rates, authorization turnaround and payment integrity without abrading providers or members.

How a health payer actually runs

In health insurance, the payer sits between clinical reality and administrative machinery, and the machinery starts at enrollment. An 834 transaction adds a member; the core platform writes the benefits, builds the accumulators and assigns the network. A provider submits an 837 claim through a clearinghouse; the gateway validates companion-guide compliance, the front-end edits test the claim structure, and the adjudication engine prices it against fee schedules, benefit logic and accumulators before the 835 remittance goes back with line-level detail. When care needs approval first, a 278 prior-authorization request opens a utilization-management case that must gather clinical evidence, apply medical policy and answer inside state and CMS turnaround clocks.

Around the claim loop, four secondary machines run continuously. Payment integrity tests every payment for coding conflicts, unbundling and unit errors, before and after payment leaves. Risk adjustment extracts HCC-eligible conditions from claims, pharmacy and chart data, because the revenue for a Medicare Advantage or ACA book depends on what was captured, not what was treated. Quality programs assemble HEDIS measure gaps from the same signals and push them to outreach. Care management stratifies members by risk and runs the programs the quality and utilization signals feed. Each machine reads the claim stream but maintains its own evidence, which is why the same clinical story gets re-collected four times.

The compliance layer wraps all of it. CMS program audits request documentation samples with short clocks; RADV audits test risk-adjustment coding against medical records; state DOI rules govern utilization management timeliness and appeal handling; NCQA accredits the processes; the MLR rebate calculation turns on every dollar classified correctly at year end. None of these can be retrofitted from a spreadsheet a week before the deadline — they read the operational record as it was made, which is why the record has to be made deliberately.

Where the money and the compliance leak

Ask payer operations and finance leaders where the money leaks and the answers converge on the same eight places, described in their own words. Dataq Software scores each of them in your estate during the diagnostic, because the leak that dominates one plan is often minor on the next — and the compliance exposure is rarely where the org chart thinks it is.

  • Prior-authorization ping-pong

    Requests bounce for missing clinical evidence that already exists in notes, portals and faxes nobody indexed. Turnaround clocks breach, providers escalate to regulators, and each retry costs a nurse reviewer hours that the decision did not need. The fix is evidence assembly, not another portal.

  • Coding integrity gaps between claim and chart

    The 837 tells a coded story; the clinical narrative tells another. Front-end edits catch structural errors but not clinical ones, so coding conflicts flow through to payment and surface later as payment-integrity recoveries, provider disputes or audit findings.

  • Risk-adjustment capture missed quietly

    HCC-eligible conditions go uncoded because no chart review ever looked, or coded without support because a coder worked from an old summary. The revenue effect is silent until RADV; the compliance effect lands on the same file.

  • Payment integrity tuned to deny, not to recover

    Edit tables tuned for catch rate generate false denials that abrade providers and raise complaint volume, while the expensive abuse — subtle upcoding, unbundling, impossible day counts — passes through clean-looking claims. Precision, not volume, is the lever nobody owns.

  • Appeals overturned on evidence never surfaced

    An appeal is lost because the determination letter could not cite the policy passage or the clinical evidence the reviewer actually relied on. Every overturned appeal is a re-worked decision plus a regulator-visible complaint, and the pattern repeats because nothing feeds back.

  • Provider data rot breaking everything downstream

    A stale provider record misroutes claims, breaks network adequacy math and publishes a wrong directory entry — three failures from one source. Directory accuracy has become a regulatory product in its own right, and it decays faster than any annual cleanup.

  • Enrollment and redetermination churn

    834 feeds, Medicaid redetermination cycles and eligibility mismatches create members who are covered in one system and not in another. Claims deny against dead eligibility, members get serviced against wrong benefits, and reconciliation happens at the finance layer, months late.

  • Audit evidence assembled under deadline

    Program audit requests, RADV samples and market-conduct exams all read the operational record, and the record was never designed for retrieval. Every audit becomes a bespoke extraction project that consumes a team and still misses context.

The pattern across all eight leaks: the payer keeps re-collecting the same clinical story because its systems never share an evidence layer. Every additional collection is cost, delay and a fresh chance for inconsistency — and inconsistency is exactly what auditors and appeals officers find.

The builds: six deep engineering services for health payers

The catalog below is the whole of our health-payer practice. Each build is named for the workflow it changes, sized in the diagnostic, and shipped with the controls its regulator expects. None of them require replacing the claims core; every one of them requires taking the data contracts and the PHI boundary seriously.

1. Claims adjudication and payment integrity — the 837-to-835 pipeline, engineered

The build starts where the claim starts: the 837 lands through the clearinghouse, and the pipeline validates companion-guide compliance at segment level, extracts the coded story — ICD-10-CM diagnoses, CPT and HCPCS procedures, units, place of service, NDC where drugs are involved — and reconciles it against the member's eligibility snapshot, the accumulators and the provider's network status before the pricing engine runs. Clean claims continue to auto-adjudication; suspect claims land in an examiner console with the conflict already located and the policy citation attached.

Payment integrity sits behind the payment rather than in front of it. Pre-payment edits are tuned against deny-rate telemetry so they catch coding conflicts — unbundling, mutually exclusive pairs, medically unlikely units — without abrading providers with false denials, and post-payment review samples by risk rather than at random. Every edit carries its rationale, so a provider appeal is answered from evidence the system already holds. The measurable deltas are auto-adjudication rate, edit precision at fixed catch rate, and dollars recovered per analyst hour.

2. Prior-authorization evidence assembly — the 278 clock, engineered

The prior-auth build attacks the ping-pong at its source: evidence assembly. A 278 request or portal referral opens a case; the pipeline reads the clinical record — chart notes, labs, imaging reports, prior claims history, pharmacy fills — maps it against the medical policy and evidence-based criteria for the requested service, and assembles the submission a nurse reviewer actually needs. Requests that unambiguously meet criteria flow to an auto-approve lane with the citation attached; everything else lands on a reviewer desk with the evidence collated and the criteria checklist pre-scored.

The human gate is a design principle: determinations are made by licensed reviewers, and the system guarantees their decision cites the policy passage and the clinical evidence it relied on, in the letter, at the time. Turnaround clocks are tracked per case against state and CMS requirements, peer-to-peer conversations are scheduled with context, and an appeal re-opens the same evidence chain rather than starting from scratch. The measurable deltas are first-pass approval rate, turnaround time and the ping-pong retries that never happen.

3. Risk adjustment and quality measurement — HCC capture and HEDIS gaps, engineered

Risk adjustment is a documentation problem wearing an actuarial costume. The build assembles candidate condition gaps from claims, pharmacy and lab signals; generates chart-chase packets per member with the specific evidence a coder needs; and routes coders through a workbench where every HCC assignment carries its source citation and confidence. Submission files are validated before they leave, and the evidence for each submitted code is stored in the exact shape a RADV auditor requests — because the audit is not an if, it is a when.

Quality measurement rides the same rails: HEDIS specifications are encoded as versioned, testable logic against the same member and claim data, hybrid-measure chart retrieval is assembled by the pipeline instead of by chart chases, and gap outreach lists flow to care management deduplicated and prioritized. The guardrail is coding integrity by design — the build never manufactures a condition the record does not support — because over-coding risk is a compliance exposure, not a revenue strategy. The measurable deltas are validated HCC capture, gap closure rate and chart retrieval effort.

4. FWA detection and SIU — network analytics on the claim stream

Fraud, waste and abuse hide in patterns a rule table cannot hold: provider-member-employer graphs, impossible geography, shared devices and bank accounts, billing rhythms that match no schedule of care. The build runs an ensemble over the claim stream — policy edits for the mechanical errors, anomaly models for the statistical outliers, network analytics for the organised patterns — and scores into an SIU workbench where the case arrives pre-assembled: linked entities, claim history, provider profile, prior referrals and the rationale for the referral itself.

The guardrails matter as much as the scoring. Referral is always a human decision with a recorded rationale; member-facing actions are gated by policy; and SIU outcomes feed back into threshold reviews, so referral precision is managed as deliberately as catch rate. Regulator-ready reporting — referral volumes, precision, recovery — comes from the same store. The measurable deltas are referral precision, recovery per referral and the false-positive cost that honest providers currently pay in friction.

5. The member decision lakehouse — enrollment, provider and care signals unified

Underneath every UM, care and integrity decision sits the same missing asset: one governed view of the member, the provider and the money. The build is a lakehouse fed by change-data-capture from claims, 834 enrollment, pharmacy, lab, care management and CRM, with schema contracts and freshness SLAs on every feed. Conformed member and provider masters apply survivorship rules your data owners sign — and in this sub-industry the provider master is a regulatory product, because directory accuracy and network adequacy both hang off it.

On top of it, the secondary machines stop re-collecting: risk stratification reads current utilization and SDOH features, outreach lists deduplicate across programs, MLR analytics connect cost per member to the program decisions that moved it, and reconciliation to finance runs as a monitored query. This is the build every other build leans on — the claims pipeline, the auth build and the quality build all read from it — which is why the diagnostic usually prices it first.

6. Appeals, grievances and grounded member servicing

Appeals are where weak decisions become regulator-visible failures. The build assembles the appeal packet from the evidence store — the original claim or determination, the policy passages, the clinical record, the reviewer's citations — so an appeal is answered from the same chain the decision made, not from a re-collection. Determination and appeal letters are generated grounded in policy language, regulatory clocks are tracked per case against CMS and state DOI requirements, and overturn patterns feed back into the criteria and edits that produced the original decision.

Member servicing rides the same foundation as a retrieval-grounded assistant: it answers benefit, network and claim-status questions from your benefit documents, formulary and directory — with citations — and hands transactional intents like PCP changes or ID card requests to governed APIs by policy. Escalations arrive at the agent desktop with context assembled. Containment is measured against post-contact outcomes, not against deflection alone, because a contained wrong answer is the most expensive kind.

The data plane: from claim fragments to a governed evidence layer

Every decision gap in a health plan traces back to a data gap, and in payers the gaps have a distinctive shape: the claim stream is structured and fast, while the clinical evidence that grounds the hardest decisions is unstructured, external and late. The data plane build handles both ends. Claims, enrollment, pharmacy and provider events land through change-data-capture into a governed lakehouse in near real time, while chart documents, FHIR resources and portal evidence land through an ingestion layer that classifies, extracts and indexes them against the member — with PHI boundaries enforced at the pipeline, not by policy memo.

Governance is engineered, not asserted. Every feed carries a schema contract with a named owner, a freshness SLA and a reconciliation check against the source system's own controls. The conformed member and provider masters apply survivorship rules signed off by your data owners, and every downstream consumer — UM criteria evaluation, payment integrity features, HEDIS logic, audit extracts — reads through the contract layer, so a schema change fails loudly at the contract instead of silently inside a measure. PHI minimization and purpose limitation are enforced as pipeline properties, which is what makes the audit conversations short.

  • Streaming claim and enrollment ingestion

    change-data-capture from adjudication, 834 eligibility and provider events with exactly-once semantics, keeping UM and integrity decisions current without touching finance batch

  • Chart evidence ingestion with PHI scoping

    documents and FHIR resources classified, extracted and indexed per member, with access scoped to the case and purpose recorded at read time

  • Conformed member and provider masters

    survivorship rules signed off by data owners; directory and network adequacy consumers read the same governed provider record the claims engine uses

  • Contract-enforced freshness

    per-feed SLAs with alerting and automated breach reports, so a late lab load pages an owner instead of silently skewing a quality measure

The decision layer: criteria, replay and reason codes

A payer runs on decisions with legal and clinical tails: an adverse determination carries appeal and regulator obligations, a claim denial carries provider and member obligations, a care-program assignment carries conduct duties. The decision layer we build treats those tails as first-class requirements. Every decision records its inputs, feature values, criteria versions, policy citations and reason codes in a replay store that answers an appeal, an audit or a member's question as a query. When anyone asks why the service was denied, the answer is retrieved — with the policy passage attached — not reconstructed.

Two governance rules are non-negotiable in this sub-industry. Automated scoring may prioritize, assemble and flag, but determinations that require clinical judgment are made by licensed reviewers whose citation obligation the system enforces; and every model or criteria version change passes through a registry your compliance and UM committees already own, with challenger evaluation on shadow data before promotion. The same decision layer hosts prior-auth triage, payment-integrity edits, FWA scoring and care-program assignment, so the governance surface is one registry and one replay store rather than four platforms with four audit stories.

The agentic layer: digital workers with audit trails

The backlog work in a payer — chart retrieval, evidence collation, gap-list assembly, audit packet preparation — follows patterns an agentic workforce can absorb without touching clinical judgment. Dataq Software's agent platform runs digital workers with explicit role scopes: the systems they may read, the tools they may call, the actions that require a human checkpoint and the budget of work per run. Every run logs its steps, tool calls and outputs to the same evidence store the human workflow uses, so an agent's file is auditable exactly like a nurse reviewer's.

Where generative agents add leverage — summarising clinical evidence for a reviewer, drafting appeal responses from the structured record, assembling chart-chase packets — the pattern is grounded generation with citation obligations and human authorship of record. Agents never make determinations, never send member or provider communications without review, and never modify coded data; they move the work to the decision point and stop. The capacity story is measured in cleared cases per reviewer per day, which is how clinical operations leaders justify the program upward.

The regulatory surface of a health payer is dense, and it reads operational records directly: HIPAA safeguards, CMS program audit and RADV, state DOI utilization-management and appeal rules, NCQA accreditation, no-surprises-billing duties, and privacy regimes for the data estate. Dataq Software's answer is the evidence layer — the artifacts an auditor will ask for are generated continuously by the systems themselves. Integration engineering: the estate stays, the contracts change

Payer estates are layered over decades, and the fastest way to fail a program is to promise greenfield. Our integration posture is contractual: we define schema contracts with each system owner, land data through CDC and governed APIs, write back through the claims, UM and CRM interfaces your change process already controls, and keep every new component independently deployable behind feature flags. Claims cores are not replaced; they are supplied with better context and drained of the decision work they were never good at.

The external chain gets the same treatment. Clearinghouses, chart-retrieval vendors, pharmacy and lab feeds, telehealth and care-management platforms sit behind an abstraction layer with contract tests, so a vendor swap is a configuration change with a migration runbook rather than a re-platform. Where a legacy adjudication component must stay, the offload pattern applies: the batch cycle keeps posting, while the decision surfaces read from the stream the offload produces. This is unglamorous work, and it is the reason the builds above hold up in production instead of in a pilot.

  • Write-back through your interfaces

    determinations, edits, care assignments and status updates flow through the claims, UM and CRM interfaces your change process already governs

  • Clearinghouse and vendor abstraction

    clearinghouse, retrieval, pharmacy and lab signals behind one contract-tested interface so a vendor swap is configuration, not surgery

  • FHIR and chart-offload pattern

    EHR evidence lands through governed ingestion with PHI scoping, keeping clinical context current without a point-to-point integration per use case

  • Feature-flagged, independently deployable

    every build ships behind flags with rehearsed rollback, because the change advisory board will ask

How an engagement runs

The engagement shape is deliberately short on ceremony and long on measurement. It starts with the two-week diagnostic: one workflow baselined end to end, the leak quantified in your own telemetry, the data contracts inventoried, and a written feasibility read you can circulate — including the uncomfortable findings. If the delta is real, the build is scoped against the gates your change, compliance and model-risk processes define; if it is not, you keep the measurement design and the baseline either way.

  • Weeks 1-2: diagnostic and baseline

    workflow instrumentation, leak quantification, contract inventory, feasibility read with the delta estimate and the confounders named

  • Weeks 3-8: first vertical slice

    one lane live behind flags with replay and evidence running from day one — for prior auth that is one service family; for payment integrity, one edit domain

  • Weeks 9-16: gates, controls, expansion

    model-risk documentation landed, QC sampling live, remaining lanes added, capacity telemetry reporting to the clinical operations review

  • Quarterly: challenger reviews

    edit and model challengers evaluated in the registry, criteria versions re-validated, the baseline re-verified against the control group

Delivery runs in your delivery rhythm, not a parallel one: our engineers work in your repositories or a shared workspace with the same CI, the same security review and the same access model your teams use — with PHI access scoped and logged from the first day. Knowledge transfer is part of the gates, not a closing slide — by the expansion phase your engineers and clinical reviewers are operating the builds, because the runbooks, contracts and evaluation suites are theirs from week one.

The team and the operating rhythm

A health-payer build team is small and senior: a lead engineer who has run decision systems in regulated health environments, data engineers who own the CDC and contract layer, ML engineers who own scoring and evaluation, a controls engineer who owns the evidence layer and the PHI boundary, and a clinical-operations lead who has run UM, quality or audit programs in production plans. Agentic-workflow specialists join for the evidence-assembly builds. The team works to your release calendar and your gate vocabulary from the first week.

The rhythm is the same at every client: weekly operations review on the workflow's own telemetry, fortnightly gate submissions to change and model-risk, quarterly challenger reviews, and an incident practice that treats the replay store and the evidence layer as production-critical systems — because by the second quarter, they are. Escalations go to named engineers, not to a queue.

Failure modes we design against

Programs like these fail in known ways, and in health payers the failure modes have a clinical and compliance flavor that banking-style automation lacks. We name them in the feasibility read and design against them explicitly, because a program that cannot survive its second quarter is not worth starting.

  • Auto-denial by stealth

    automation quietly shifting from assembling evidence to making denials — the design rule is that clinical determinations stay with licensed reviewers, and the replay store proves it did

  • Provider abrasion

    edit precision collapsing into false denials that raise complaints and push providers to regulators — precision at fixed catch rate is a tracked metric with a floor, not an aspiration

  • Coding-integrity drift

    risk-adjustment capture improving on paper while unsupported codes accumulate — the coder workbench stores evidence per code precisely so RADV reads the truth, not a story

  • PHI sprawl

    evidence copies leaking into pipelines, notebooks and exports outside the governed estate — PHI scoping is a pipeline property with access analytics, not a policy document

  • Model-risk stall

    the scoring or retrieval service stuck in review because documentation was an afterthought — the registry, evaluation results and lineage land with the build, not after it

  • Pilot purgatory

    a demo built on extracted claims data that never integrates — we ship the first vertical slice through your change process, flags and gates included, within the first two months

Questions payer leaders ask us

  • Does this touch clinical judgment?

    No. The builds assemble evidence, score and prioritize; licensed reviewers make determinations, and the system enforces their citation obligation. Every automated decision is scoped, logged and replayable, and the boundary is visible in the replay store.

  • How does this pass HIPAA review?

    PHI scoping is enforced at the pipeline level, access is logged with purpose, BAAs are inventoried per integration, and the evidence layer generates the access analytics a security or privacy review asks for as a query rather than a project.

  • Do we need to replace our claims platform first?

    No. The builds consume streams from the claims core, the UM platform and the gateway, and write back through the interfaces your change process governs. The core stays; it stops being the bottleneck for decisions.

  • Will providers see more denials?

    The opposite is the design goal: tuned edits and pre-assembled evidence mean fewer false denials, faster first-pass approvals and letters that cite the policy passage — the friction providers complain about is precisely what the build removes.

  • How do you measure success without gaming?

    Baselines are locked during the diagnostic, controls or holdouts are used where experiments are cheap, and every metric reports from system telemetry rather than a status deck. The feasibility read names the confounders before anyone commits to a target.

  • What is the smallest program worth running?

    One workflow, one baseline, one vertical slice — prior authorization and payment integrity are the two most common starters because their clocks and edit telemetry are already instrumented. If the diagnostic cannot find the delta, we say so and stop.

  • How do the agents avoid becoming shadow AI?

    Agents run inside the governed platform with role scopes, tool allow-lists, human checkpoints and full run logs. The shadow-AI failure mode is what happens without those controls; the program's explicit goal is to eliminate the conditions that create it.

  • Who owns the build after you leave?

    Your teams, with the runbooks, contracts, evaluation suites and registry entries transferred as gate conditions during expansion — not as a closing deliverable. Several programs run entirely in-house after the second quarter.
