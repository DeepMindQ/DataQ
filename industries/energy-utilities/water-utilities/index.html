Water, Waste & City Utilities

A water utility sells one product and loses a measurable share of it before a customer ever sees it. Non-revenue water — leakage, metering error, unauthorized use — absorbs a fifth of production in many networks, and recovering part of that gap pays for the entire instrumented program. Around the loss sit the other duties: keep chlorine residual inside limits at the tap, keep overflow events inside permit, keep pumping inside an energy budget, keep the billing chain honest, and keep renewing a buried asset base whose condition nobody can see. This page is the engineering picture of that operation: the systems that run it, the exact points where water, energy and compliance leak, and the six deep builds Dataq Software ships to close them.

How the network runs day to day

A water utility moves one product through a network it can only partly see. Treated water leaves the works under SCADA control — dosing, filtration, clear-water storage, high-lift pumping — and fans out through trunk mains, service reservoirs and district metered areas toward the tap. The utility's picture of that journey is deliberately sparse: flow and pressure at DMA inlets, a handful of quality probes, acoustic loggers that wake when a leak sounds, and smart meters wherever AMI has reached. Everything else is inferred from balances. When a district's minimum night flow rises, the water is going somewhere; the question that decides the margin is how quickly that somewhere gets a name, an estimate and a work order.

The operating day is a sequence of balances and exceptions. Night teams review district flows against minimum-night-flow baselines and step-test zones by closing valves; day crews clear leak and repair work orders ranked by whatever evidence reached them; the control room answers alarms from treatment SCADA and reservoir telemetry. Water quality sampling runs to a regulatory calendar — residual chlorine at defined points, turbidity, pH, bacteriology — with laboratory results landing in LIMS days after the water has been drunk. Customer contacts cluster on billing surprises and supply interruptions, and each one is a chance to reconcile meter-to-bill chains that drift quietly. The sewer estate adds its own exceptions: level monitors rising toward surcharge, spill events against permits, jetting crews dispatched by complaint.

Underneath, the machinery is an asset register wearing telemetry. GIS holds the mains, valves, hydrants and service connections; the CMMS holds repair histories that would tell a burst-prediction model everything it needs if anyone could parse them; hydraulic models of the EPANET class simulate zones the calibration data only roughly describes. Capital planning runs on a renewal cycle negotiated between pipe age, break anecdotes and tolerance for street disruption. Regulatory reporting assembles itself from sampling schedules and telemetry exports that nobody owns end to end. The result is a business with abundant measurements and few decisions connected to them — which is precisely the gap an engineering program can close.

The estate underneath: systems this work touches

Programs here succeed or fail on how honestly the estate is described before anything is built. The layers below are the ones a water utility actually runs — named as the network team would name them — together with the point where each one strains under real throughput, real weather and real regulatory scrutiny.

Where the margin leaks

Ask operations leaders where the money leaks in water and the answers converge on the same eight places, described in their own words. Dataq Software scores each of them in your estate during the diagnostic, because the leak that dominates one network is rarely the one that dominates the next.

  • Non-revenue water unbudgeted and unlocated

    Leakage runs as a percentage nobody can decompose: district balances are stale, step tests are episodic, and acoustic surveys chase noise. Night-flow data exists but reaches analysts in spreadsheets days late. The result is reactive main-break response instead of targeted leak reduction, and a regulatory leakage number defended with assumptions rather than evidence. Recovering even part of the gap funds the whole program.

  • Pressure zones managed by complaint

    Pressure management is the cheapest leakage and main-break lever in the network, yet pressure-reducing valve setpoints are tuned by history rather than by live hydraulics. Transients go unrecorded; the zones that surge every night keep breaking quietly. Without pressure analytics the utility pays twice — in water lost through smaller fractures and in assets aged faster than the renewal plan assumes.

  • Burst prediction without break history

    Every main break is a data point recorded as a free-text work order. Pipe material, install decade, soil, prior failures and pressure exposure never meet in one model, so renewal priorities are argued from age alone. The mains that will fail this winter are knowable; the register that would say so is buried in narratives nobody has time to parse.

  • Quality caught by probe, proven by paper

    Online chlorine and turbidity probes stream continuously while the regulatory case is built from laboratory samples that arrive days later. Excursions are caught, but the evidence chain — probe reading to corrective action to laboratory confirmation — is assembled by hand for every audit. Compliance becomes a quarterly scramble instead of a property of the operating system.

  • Energy burned by pumps on yesterday's schedule

    Pumping is typically the largest controllable cost in the business, and pump schedules follow habits set years ago against tariffs that have changed since. Affinity curves, reservoir bands and time-of-use prices would let the optimizer shift megalitres into cheap hours; instead stations run flat and the energy line grows. The physics is known; the decision layer is missing.

  • Meter-to-bill chains that drift in silence

    Stuck meters, mis-sized meters, estimated reads and tariff misclassifications surface only when customers complain. Consumption analytics that would flag the vacancy, the leak-through and the dead meter before the bill exists are absent. Revenue leakage here is individually small and collectively material, and every disputed bill costs more to resolve than to prevent.

  • Sewer spills discovered by the public

    Overflow and sewer-level monitoring is sparse, so surcharge and spill events are discovered by residents or by the regulator rather than by telemetry. Event evidence for permits is reconstructed after the fact, from memory and rain charts. Each undocumented event is a compliance exposure, and each reactive callout is a crew spent on a problem telemetry could have ranked.

  • Capital renewal argued from pipe age

    The capital program is negotiated between install dates, break anecdotes and disruption tolerance. Because the asset register and the repair history never meet, the true risk ranking — likelihood of failure multiplied by consequence — is computed nowhere. Money lands on the mains that shout loudest politically, and the quiet arterial main with the worst records waits.

The pattern across all eight leaks: the network is instrumented in fragments and the decisions are made from balances nobody trusts. Every leak is a data-contract problem before it is a modeling problem — which is why Dataq Software's first build in water is almost always the network data fabric, not a smarter leak model.

The builds: six deep engineering services for water and waste utilities

The catalog below is the whole of our water practice. Each build is a named engineering service with defined data flows, decision services, guardrails and integration points — deployed against your estate in weeks, and designed so the evidence the regulator wants falls out of running the system.

1. Non-revenue water analytics — DMA balances to leak work orders

The build reconstructs the water balance district by district: DMA inlet metering validated at source, nightly minimum-night-flow baselines computed and maintained, step-test records structured, and supply-pipe leakage separated from meter under-registration and unauthorized use. Estimates carry confidence intervals so the leakage number can be defended to the regulator and to finance. Leak candidates are ranked by run rate multiplied by likely duration — the economics of water saved against cost to save — and pushed into the crew schedule with location hypotheses and pressure context, not as a dashboard the team must interpret.

Decision services: nightly district exception reports, leak-candidate worklists sequenced by economics, step-test plans that concentrate measurement where the balance is weakest, and repair verification that closes the loop when the flow comes down. Guardrails: no district enters scoring until its metering has passed a data-quality gate; every balance shows its inputs so an auditor can replay it. Integration points: DMA loggers, AMI intervals, billing consumption, CMMS repair records. The published record for this pattern shows non-revenue water down twenty-one percent at a national utility — measured, not modeled.

2. Pressure management and burst prediction

The build joins live zone pressure, pressure-reducing valve positions and telemetry to calibrated hydraulic models, so the network team sees surge events, night-time excess pressure and dead-end stagnation as they happen rather than as break statistics afterwards. PRV setpoints are optimized against the twin objectives of leakage and burst frequency, with transients flagged from high-frequency pressure logging where installed. Zones without adequate sensing are named as measurement gaps and sequenced into the instrumentation plan rather than quietly excluded from the program.

The burst-prediction layer consumes what the estate already knows: main-break history parsed from CMMS free text, pipe material and vintage from GIS, soil and weather context, and live pressure exposure from the zones above. The output is a ranked failure forecast per main — likelihood times consequence — feeding both the renewal plan and the winter readiness plan. Guardrails: predictions never move a valve; crews and engineers approve; every forecast carries its evidence so a planner can interrogate it. Integration points: pressure sensors, PRV controllers, GIS, CMMS, the hydraulic model library.

3. Water quality and environmental compliance — probe to permit

The build fuses online probes — chlorine residual, turbidity, pH, conductivity — with the laboratory sampling calendar into one quality picture per zone. Exceedances trigger the corrective-action workflow automatically: flush orders, re-probing, laboratory confirmation and the notification evidence the regulator expects. Sampling schedules are tracked as deadlines rather than documents, so a missed sample is an alert, not a discovery. For the sewer estate, level telemetry classifies surcharge and spill events against permit conditions and assembles the event dossier — timestamps, volumes, rainfall context, actions taken — while the event is fresh.

Decision services: exceedance worklists with response steps attached, sample-schedule compliance telemetry, spill-event dossiers generated from telemetry and crew records, and trend screens that catch slow deterioration before it becomes an excursion. Guardrails: probe readings used for compliance always reconcile to laboratory truth on a defined cadence; customer notifications route through your communications team; no dossier leaves without its provenance chain. Integration points: treatment SCADA, LIMS, sewer level monitors, rainfall feeds, permit registers. The compliance story becomes a query — which is what makes audits short.

4. Pump scheduling and energy optimization

The build models each pump station from its own data: affinity curves fitted from telemetry, efficiency maps per duty point, wear signatures that shift the curves over time. The optimizer schedules high-lift and booster pumping against reservoir bands, demand forecasts, time-of-use tariffs and demand-response obligations, shifting megalitres into cheap hours without breaching level or pressure constraints. Works processes — backwash, dosing, filtration cycles — join the same energy account so the whole of the kilowatt-hours per megalitre is visible and owned.

Decision services: day-ahead pump schedules with tariff and reservoir context, real-time re-optimization when demand or availability moves, station efficiency reports that name the pumps drifting off their curves, and energy-per-megalitre baselined by zone and works. Guardrails: schedules are proposals until the control room accepts them; hard constraints — reservoir floors, pressure minima, standby duty — are architectural; every schedule stores the constraints active at the time for replay. Integration points: SCADA, tariff and settlement feeds, demand forecasts, works historians. The delta lands on the energy line and stays there.

5. Meter-to-bill integrity and AMI analytics

The build closes the gap between the meter and the invoice. AMI intervals, read cycles and billing events land in one pipeline with anomaly detection that flags the stuck meter, the reversal, the consumption collapse that means a leak-through or a vacancy, and the mis-sized meter running at the bottom of its range. Cases route to the right resolution path — swap order, bill adjustment, customer contact — with the evidence pre-assembled, so revenue leakage is found before the complaint rather than by it.

The same intervals power the customer-facing work: consumption analytics for conservation and drought messaging, tariff simulation for affordability reviews, and vulnerability-aware treatment for hardship programs so enforcement never lands on the households least able to respond. Guardrails: meter data carries its privacy contract end to end; estimates are labelled as estimates in every downstream surface; billing corrections reconcile to the ledger before they post. Integration points: AMI head-end, billing, contact center, vulnerability registers. The measurable deltas are billing accuracy, dispute volume and revenue protected per year.

6. Asset renewal and capital prioritization

The build turns the asset register and the repair history into a renewal case that survives a finance committee. Mains, valves, hydrants and service connections are scored on likelihood of failure — from break history, material, vintage, soil and pressure exposure — and on consequence of failure, from criticality, customers affected and repair cost. The output is a ranked, risked renewal program with deferrable and non-deferrable classes, so capital lands where the network risk actually is, and every deferral decision carries its evidence.

Decision services: renewal rankings by risked consequence, intervention-versus-replacement economics per main cohort, disruption-scheduled programs that cluster works by street, and post-renewal verification that shows the break curve bending. Guardrails: scoring weights are owned and signed by engineering; the register's data gaps are published rather than papered over; every ranking is replayable for the regulator who funds it. Integration points: GIS, CMMS, billing exposure, hydraulic criticality from the models. The delta is capital efficiency — the same budget, measurably lower network risk — reported from the system, not the slide deck.

The data plane: telemetry truth with the register attached

Every decision gap in water traces back to a data gap, and most of those gaps are engineering choices rather than physics. Telemetry arrives at native cadence but without the district, zone or asset it belongs to; the GIS holds the network geometry with attributes of uneven trust; the CMMS holds the repair reality as free text; billing holds consumption that never meets the network view. The data plane's job is to make those four views queryable together at decision speed — schema contracts on every feed, the asset-register spine as the join, and quality gates that quarantine a stuck logger instead of letting it skew a district balance.

Governance is engineered, not asserted. Every value in the lakehouse can name its source system, transformation chain and quality state at read time, which is what makes the evidence layer possible later. Logger batteries, comms dropouts and clock drift are detected at ingest with gap metadata attached, so a district balance degrades honestly instead of confidently. Laboratory results join online probes as first-class feeds with chain of custody, because the compliance decisions this industry makes are always conditional on measurements someone signed.

  • Native-cadence telemetry ingestion

    DMA loggers, pressure sensors and treatment SCADA stream at their native rates with backfill handled explicitly and gap metadata attached; the control path is never touched and the historian keeps its role while the lakehouse adds the context it lacks

  • The asset-register spine

    every flow, pressure, quality reading and work order tied to the main, valve, meter or zone that produced it, signed off by the engineers who own the register — context bound as an engineering contract, not a tribal convention

  • Quality gates with named owners

    flatlines, clock drift and stuck values detected at ingest; the affected feature set quarantined and the owner paged, so a dead logger pages a technician rather than quietly skewing a night-flow baseline

  • Billing-consumption reconciliation

    AMI intervals reconciled to the billing view on a defined cadence, with estimated reads labelled and meter-to-bill exceptions surfaced as a worklist rather than as complaints weeks later

The decision layer: bounded hydraulics with full replay

Water runs on decisions with physical, financial and regulatory tails: a PRV setpoint moved, a district opened for step-testing, a spill event classified, a pump schedule committed, a renewal deferred. The decision layer treats every one as a first-class object — inputs, model version, calibrated envelope, constraints active, expected delta, human approval — stored in a replay service that can re-derive any decision months later for an auditor, a regulator or an engineer asking what the system knew at the time. Champion and challenger run permanently: every leakage model, burst forecast and pump schedule competes against the incumbent on locked baselines before it earns traffic.

The guardrails are as engineered as the models. Recommendations are bounded by calibrated model envelopes and sensor coverage, and anything approaching a boundary escalates with its reasoning attached. Decision rights are explicit — what the optimizer may draft, what the control room must approve, what stays with the crew on the ground. Uncertainty travels forward rather than being averaged away: a night-flow anomaly without its meter-confidence caveat never reaches a leak analyst, and a spill classification without its rainfall context never reaches a permit return. The result is optimization the compliance team and the finance committee can both interrogate.

The agentic layer: governed digital workers for network paperwork

Most repeat work in a water utility is investigative paperwork: assembling leak-investigation packs, reconciling sampling schedules against returns, drafting spill-event dossiers, parsing free-text repair records into structured histories, preparing regulatory submissions. These are retrieval, assembly and cross-checking jobs with clear success criteria — ideal for governed agents that work inside the platform with role scopes, tool allow-lists and complete run logs. Each agent ships with an evaluation suite: golden cases where the right answer is known, adversarial cases where the tempting answer is wrong, and a human checkpoint at every boundary that matters.

Where generative capability adds leverage — summarizing district histories, drafting narrative sections of regulatory returns, translating inspection PDFs into structured records — it operates with retrieval grounding against your estate and provenance stamps on every claim. No agent closes a valve, dispatches a crew or files a compliance return; those boundaries are architectural. The agentic layer's output is reviewed work moving faster, with the run log to show for it — and when the regulator asks who classified the event, the answer is a person, on the record.

Integration engineering: the network stays, the contracts change

Water estates are layered over decades — SCADA from several vendors, loggers bought campaign by campaign, a GIS assembled from merged utilities, billing systems that predate AMI — and a program that demands greenfield first will never start. Dataq Software wraps the estate instead: adapters at native cadence, normalizers for logger and head-end formats, schema contracts that make each upstream system answerable for freshness and shape. Control systems are read-first by policy; nothing writes into a controlled system without your change process, and most builds never need to.

The supply chain gets the same treatment. Logger vendors, meter head-ends, laboratory networks, demand-forecasting services and weather feeds all become contract parties to the data plane: formats specified, freshness SLAs enforced, test harnesses run per delivery. When a vendor changes a format, the contract test fails before the decision layer notices. The register gets its own program within the program: attribute repair, valve topology and free-text work orders structured to the point where analytics can consume them.

  • Read-first SCADA integration

    every path into works and network control starts read-only at native cadence; the rare write path goes through your change process with your engineers owning the change from design to verification

  • Logger and head-end normalizers

    DMA logger exports, AMI head-end feeds and laboratory result files normalized at ingest with schema contracts and golden-file tests per vendor release

  • Register and CMMS structure repair

    asset attributes, valve topology and free-text repair histories rebuilt to analytics grade in the first quarter — data debt named as a gate condition with acceptance criteria, not discovered mid-program

  • Model-library bridges

    EPANET-class hydraulic models fed live telemetry through governed interfaces, so the calibrated model the studies use matches the network the operators actually see

The team and the operating rhythm

Agentic-workflow specialists join for the evidence-assembly builds. The rhythm is the same at every client: a weekly operations review on the metric, a monthly delta review against the locked baseline with finance in the room, and quarterly challenger sessions where leakage models, burst forecasts and pump schedules defend their traffic. Runbooks, evaluation suites and the decision-replay store transfer to your teams as gate conditions during expansion — the program's dependency on us is scheduled to shrink, and the schedule is visible to everyone.

Failure modes we design against

Programs in water fail in known ways, and most of the failure modes are organizational rather than hydraulic. We name them in the feasibility read and design against them explicitly, because a program that cannot survive its second summer is not worth starting.

  • Pilot purgatory

    a leak model demoed on extracted data that never lands in the estate — prevented by shipping the first vertical slice through your change process within two months, flags and gates included

  • Unvalidated metering at the core

    district balances scored on meters nobody has proven — every district passes a data-quality gate before scoring, and the gaps are sequenced into the instrumentation plan

  • Leakage figures defended by assumption

    the reported number argued from factors rather than balances — figures are computed from telemetry with confidence intervals and published inputs, or they are not reported

  • Instrumentation without decision loops

    loggers installed while worklists still run by complaint — sensing is sequenced where the computed delta justifies it, and each district's decision loop goes live with its meters

  • Register debt quietly compounding

    renewal analytics starved by attribute errors and free-text histories — register repair is a first-quarter gate condition with acceptance criteria the engineers sign

  • Evidence debt at audit time

    regulator returns assembled by a team every cycle because artifacts are manual — the evidence layer generates them continuously, or the build ships unfinished

Questions water operations leaders ask us

  • Do you touch the control systems?

    We read. Write paths to pumps or valves go through your change process with your engineers in the loop, and most builds never need them. Recommendations land in control-room workflows; the SCADA keeps its authority.

  • Can this work with sparse instrumentation?

    Yes — the fabric starts with what exists, and the diagnostic names the districts where metering investment pays. We sequence instrumentation like any other capital: where the computed delta justifies it.

  • How do you handle vendor-locked logger formats?

    Adapters and normalizers are part of the build; every major logger and AMI head-end format we integrate is normalized at ingest. Your licenses stay; the export contract changes so decisions can consume the data.

  • What happens to our leak-detection crews?

    Their judgment moves up a level: the build assembles the candidates and the evidence; crews confirm, excavate and own the physical decisions. Programs are justified in leaks found per crew-day, not headcount removed.

  • How does this survive a regulatory audit?

    Every artifact ships with lineage, confidence intervals and approval gates. The balance, the quality record and the event dossiers are generated continuously, so the audit reads structured records instead of assembled files.

  • Can you price against the delta rather than the effort?

    Yes, where the metric is clean. Non-revenue water, energy per megalitre and leak run time all baseline in your telemetry during the diagnostic; the feasibility read names the confounders first.

  • What is the smallest program worth running?

    One district metered area, one pressure zone or one works — whichever has the cleanest baseline. If the diagnostic cannot measure the delta in two weeks, we say so and stop. You keep the measurement design either way.

  • Who owns the models after you leave?

    Your teams, with runbooks, model documentation, evaluation suites and the decision-replay store transferred as gate conditions during expansion. Several programs run entirely in-house after the second quarter.
