Power Generation & Grid

Power system operators balance physics, markets and regulation in the same sixty seconds. The control room commits generation against a load forecast, restores feeders around a fault, activates flexibility from a rooftop fleet it cannot fully see, and defends all of it before a regulator — while intermittent supply keeps rewriting the assumptions the system was built on. The telemetry-to-decision loop is the core competency of the transition era: whoever turns synchrophasors, meter reads and market signals into dispatch decisions fastest, wins. This page is the engineering picture of that loop: the systems a grid operator actually runs, the exact points where reliability and revenue leak, and the six deep builds Dataq Software ships to close them.

How the grid operation actually runs, dispatch desk to dead-end feeder

In power system operations, the physics sets the tempo and the paperwork sets the boundaries. The transmission layer runs EMS against a network model: state estimation, load flow, contingency analysis, automatic generation control — fed by SCADA over DNP3 and, increasingly, by synchrophasors per IEEE C37.118 that let operators see oscillations instead of inferring them. The distribution layer runs ADMS and DMS: switching orders, fault location and isolation, volt-var optimization on feeders whose topology the field keeps quietly editing. Both layers feed an outage management system where storm mode turns a quiet Tuesday into three hundred crews and a public dashboard. Each layer has its own model, its own protocol stack and its own definition of now.

Underneath the control rooms, the commercial machinery never sleeps. Load and renewable forecasts drive unit commitment and day-ahead bids; intraday markets reprice the residuals; settlement then reconciles scheduled energy against metered energy and invoices the difference as imbalance. The metering estate — AMI head-ends and meter data management — produces the interval truth that settlement, planning and customer functions all consume through validation-edit-estimation rules nobody outside the metering team can explain. DER compounds the complexity: rooftop solar, batteries and electric-vehicle charging show up in hosting-capacity studies and flexibility registers, but their telemetry arrives late, partial and unverified.

Wrapped around all of it is the compliance estate: NERC CIP for bulk-power cyber duties, IEC 62443 for OT zoning, NIS2 and DORA-class resilience obligations, network codes, market conduct rules and sustainability reporting. The evidence these regimes demand — access reviews, change records, monitoring logs, restoration timelines — exists in the systems, but is assembled by hand every audit cycle. Dataq Software's work concentrates on closing the gap between the running grid and the decision layer: forecasts that feed bids directly, asset condition that feeds worklists directly, flexibility that dispatches within its documented limits, and evidence that generates itself.

The estate underneath: systems this work touches

Grid programs succeed or fail on how honestly the estate is described. The layers below are the ones an operator actually runs — named as they appear in the control room and the asset office, not as a vendor catalog would have them — together with the point where each one strains under real weather, real DER growth and real regulatory scrutiny.

Where reliability and revenue leak

Ask grid operators where the money leaks and the answers converge on the same eight places, described in their own words. Dataq Software scores each of them in your estate during the diagnostic, because the leak that dominates a transmission-heavy system is rarely the one that dominates a distribution cooperative with a fast-growing rooftop fleet.

  • Forecast error compounding into imbalance

    Legacy load and renewable forecasting runs on methods the weather has outgrown. Every miss becomes imbalance cost, a curtailment instruction issued too late, or a unit commitment that holds expensive capacity on standby. The error compounds through bidding, scheduling and settlement, and nobody can attribute the cost precisely enough to fix the worst-contributing zones and products first.

  • Substation assets uninstrumented

    Transformers, breakers and cables hold failure precursors nobody sees: dissolved gas trending toward a trip, a breaker mechanism wearing past its duty cycle, a joint heating on a summer afternoon. Inspection schedules predate the sensors, so condition-based maintenance is impossible and the failures that arrive are the unscheduled kind — the most expensive, on the assets that matter most.

  • CIP evidence assembled by hand

    NERC CIP duties — asset classification, access reviews, change records, monitoring — are satisfied annually by a team reconstructing what the systems already know. The reconstruction itself becomes the risk: evidence drifts from system state, findings repeat, and nobody can answer quickly what a compromised zone would actually have reached.

  • DER flexibility activation by phone

    Flexibility markets exist; the activation path does not. Curtailment instructions, demand response and storage dispatch get orchestrated by email, portal and phone tree, so the fastest flexibility in the portfolio is also the least usable. Aggregators deliver telemetry the operator cannot verify, and hosting-capacity studies go stale before the interconnection queue moves.

  • Unit-commitment ceilings

    Scheduling formulations exceed what the solver and the process can chew in the window available: network constraints approximated, storage modeled crudely, stochastic unit commitment abandoned for deterministic habit. The gap between the schedule the market got and the schedule the physics wanted settles later as imbalance, congestion and curtailment — costs booked far from their cause.

  • Grid-edge data blind

    AMI head-ends and DER telemetry hold the most detailed picture of the network ever produced, and planning consumes almost none of it. Voltage problems surface as complaints; transformer loading is inferred from nameplates; hosting capacity is studied from spreadsheets. The edge is instrumented and blind at the same time — the data exists, the decision pipeline does not.

  • Restoration sequencing under storm stress

    When the storm lands, restoration priorities are set by experience and phone calls, not by customers-at-risk computed live against network, weather and crew state. ETAs published to the public drift for hours; crews arrive to find the isolation already switched; the after-action review reconstructs decisions from memory. Storm cost concentrates exactly where the decision loop is slowest.

  • Vegetation spend without risk targeting

    Trimming cycles run on calendar and budget habit while outage statistics quietly rank the feeders nobody prioritizes. The line crews know which spans fail; the program does not. A decade of outage and inspection data exists to reprice every kilometre of the network, and the budget still spreads evenly where the risk does not.

The pattern across all eight leaks: the grid is instrumented but the decisions are not. Every leak is a context problem before it is a modeling problem — which is why Dataq Software's first build in grid operations is almost always the data fabric that joins EMS, MDM and asset records, not a smarter model sitting on top of a broken decision supply chain.

The builds: six deep engineering services for power generation and grid

The catalog below is the whole of our grid practice. Each build is a named engineering service with defined data flows, decision services, guardrails and integration points — deployed against your estate in weeks, not quarters, and designed so the evidence the regulator wants falls out of running the system.

1. The forecasting and imbalance decision hub

The build replaces spreadsheet forecasting with a weather-coupled decision service: load, wind, solar and DER-behind-the-meter predicted at zone, feeder and portfolio level, with confidence intervals attached and the forecast wired directly into everything that consumes it — unit commitment, day-ahead and intraday bids, storage dispatch, maintenance windows. Backcasting runs continuously against locked baselines, so forecast quality is a league table by zone and product rather than an anecdote defended quarterly. Solver tournaments evaluate scheduling formulations in parallel, because the formulation that wins at one grid topology loses at another.

Decision services: bid recommendations with risk bounds, imbalance-exposure dashboards naming the contributing feeders or farms, commitment proposals that respect network constraints and storage behavior. Guardrails: forecasts degrade gracefully with data gaps and say so; every schedule carries its inputs for replay; the operator or trader approves before anything executes. Integration points: EMS, market interfaces, weather providers, AMI and DER telemetry. This is the build behind the published record — forecast error down thirty-one percent, imbalance cost recovered across three European control zones.

2. Substation asset health — DGA, breakers and the condition ledger

The build joins transformer dissolved-gas analysis, breaker operation counters and timing traces, partial-discharge surveys, thermal imagery and loading history into a single condition ledger per asset, anchored to the asset register and the CMMS. Failure-mode models run per asset class with validated precursors — gas-ratio signatures, mechanism wear patterns, thermal thresholds — and risk-based inspection plans start consuming current condition instead of last inspection date. Monitors that were bolted on as projects become a fleet fabric that reprices the intervention list every morning.

Decision services: ranked intervention worklists with risked consequence, capital-renewal prioritization with failure cost modeled honestly, loading recommendations for transformers approaching their thermal limits. Guardrails: recommendations never bypass engineering approval; every computed health index carries its inputs for replay; assets whose sensor coverage cannot support scoring are flagged rather than scored. Integration points: CMMS, historians, condition-monitoring vendors, SCADA. The delta lands as unscheduled stoppages avoided and capital moved from calendar habit to measured risk.

3. DERMS and flexibility orchestration

The build makes DER flexibility dispatchable within its documented limits: aggregation verified against telemetry rather than assumed, curtailment instructions issued and confirmed in minutes, storage and demand-response dispatch co-optimized against market prices and network constraints. Hosting-capacity studies run continuously against real grid-edge data instead of stale load models, so the interconnection queue stops moving against yesterday's network. Flexibility registers become a live contract: what each aggregated asset can deliver, when, at what confidence, verified per event.

Decision services: flexibility dispatch recommendations with verified headroom, curtailment-minimization plays that co-optimize storage and demand response, market participation proposals priced against delivery risk. Guardrails: aggregations below telemetry confidence never dispatch; network safety limits are hard constraints the optimizer cannot trade away; every instruction and confirmation is logged for the regulator. Integration points: DERMS platforms, ADMS, market interfaces, AMI head-end. The delta lands as curtailment recovered and flexibility revenue actually captured instead of administratively forfeited.

4. Outage and storm response orchestration

The build closes the loop between the outage management system, the network model, the crew state and the weather. Restoration sequencing optimizes customers-at-risk rather than call volume; crew staging is computed against predicted fault density and road reality; public ETAs are recomputed continuously from executed work rather than estimated once and defended forever. Every switching action writes back to the network model, so the ADMS stops drifting from the field and the after-action review reads from records instead of memory.

Decision services: storm-mode crew allocation, restoration-sequence recommendations with customers-at-risk quantified, ETA recalibration as conditions move, mutual-assistance requests sized by modeled need. Guardrails: safety-critical switching stays human-gated by design; every sequence carries its network snapshot for replay; crew safety constraints override optimization always. Integration points: OMS, ADMS, mobile workforce platforms, GIS, weather services. The delta lands as SAIDI and MAIFI improvement on the storms that dominate the decade's statistics, and after-action reviews that actually improve the next response.

5. NERC CIP evidence and OT segmentation

The build treats cyber events as operational events and compliance as a property of the running system. IEC 62443 zone-and-conduit models are designed against your actual network, then enforced and monitored: OT traffic baselines per zone, drift alerts, remote access brokered through identity with sessions recorded. NERC CIP evidence — asset classification, access reviews, change records, monitoring logs — generates continuously from the systems of record, with the electronic security perimeter documented as it actually is rather than as the last assessment drew it.

Decision services: zone health dashboards, access-risk reviews prioritized by consequence, incident playbooks tied to OT scenarios, bulk-electric-system impact analysis for change requests. Guardrails: no change enters a controlled system outside MOC; segmentation designs are validated with your OT engineers before deployment; monitoring is passive-first so the control path stays untouched. Integration points: SCADA networks, historians, identity providers, GRC tooling. NIS2 and DORA-class duties land on the same evidence pipeline, because the artifacts those regimes want are the ones a healthy OT estate produces anyway.

6. Settlement, imbalance and market decision support

The build joins scheduling, metered volumes and market statements into one settlement fabric: every imbalance invoice traceable to the schedules that caused it, every ancillary revenue stream auditable to the telemetry that earned it, every curtailment claim evidenced from SCADA and meter data rather than reconstructed from spreadsheets. Validation-edit-estimation rules from the MDM become explainable artifacts, so settlement disputes resolve against a shared record instead of dueling exports from the same systems.

Decision services: imbalance cost attribution by zone, product and forecast source; settlement dispute packs assembled automatically; ancillary-services performance tracked against obligation windows; curtailment claims evidenced end to end. Guardrails: every settlement figure carries its lineage; disputes freeze their evidence pack so positions cannot drift; finance and operations read the same numbers from the same fabric. Integration points: settlement systems, MDM, market interfaces, SCADA. The delta lands as imbalance cost down, disputes settled in weeks not quarters, and ancillary revenue finally audited to source.

The data plane: protocol-native streams with the grid model attached

Every decision gap in grid operations traces back to a data gap, and most of those gaps are engineering choices rather than physics. The EMS holds the transmission truth, the ADMS the distribution truth, the AMI head-end the consumption truth — in three protocols, three models and three cadences. The data plane's job is to make those views queryable together at decision speed: IEC 61850 and DNP3 ingested at native cadence, synchrophasors streamed with their time sync intact, meter reads joined per meter point, and the network model attached as the spine so every reading lands on the feeder, bay or transformer that produced it.

Governance is engineered, not asserted. Every value in the lakehouse can name its source system, transformation chain and quality state at read time, which is what makes the evidence layer possible later. Telemetry gaps from remote telemetry units and third-party DER arrive with gap metadata attached, so forecasts and dispatch degrade honestly. Weather, market prices and vegetation data join as first-class feeds, because the decisions a grid makes are always conditional on all three.

  • Protocol-native ingestion

    IEC 61850, DNP3 and C37.118 streams consumed at their native cadence with event semantics preserved — synchrophasor time alignment verified, point maps documented, and the control path never touched

  • The network-model spine

    every reading tied to the feeder, bay or asset that produced it, reconciled to the EMS and ADMS models with field edits written back — model drift becomes a measurable, managed quantity

  • Quality gates with named owners

    staleness, flatlines and sensor drift detected at ingest; the affected feature set quarantined and the owner paged, so a stuck RTU pages a technician rather than quietly degrading a forecast

  • Grid-edge joins

    AMI interval data, DER telemetry and billing-class reads joined per meter point and rolled to transformer and feeder, so planning finally consumes the edge it already measures

The decision layer: bounded dispatch with replay

Grid operations run on decisions with regulatory tails: a bid submitted to a market, a curtailment instruction issued to a community, a switching sequence that de-energizes a street. The decision layer treats every one as a first-class object — inputs, model version, constraints active, expected delta, human approval — stored in a replay service that can re-derive any decision months later for a regulator, an after-action review or an engineer asking what the system knew at the time. Champion and challenger run permanently: every forecasting method, dispatch policy and restoration heuristic competes against the incumbent on locked baselines before it earns traffic.

The guardrails are as engineered as the models. Recommendations are bounded by validated network and safety envelopes; anything approaching a boundary escalates with its reasoning attached. Decision rights are explicit — what the system may do alone, what it drafts for approval, what stays human by regulation or choice. Uncertainty travels forward rather than being averaged away: a forecast without its confidence interval never reaches a bid, and a flexibility dispatch without its verification status never reaches a community. The result is optimization the control room, the market desk and the regulator can all interrogate.

The agentic layer: digital workers on the paperwork frontier

The backlog work in grid operations is evidentiary: assembling CIP evidence packs, preparing settlement dispute files, reconciling DER aggregation records, drafting restoration after-action reports, maintaining third-party registers. These are retrieval, assembly and cross-checking jobs with clear success criteria — ideal for governed agents that work inside the platform with role scopes, tool allow-lists and complete run logs. Each agent ships with an evaluation suite: golden cases where the right answer is known, adversarial cases where the tempting answer is wrong, and a human checkpoint at every boundary that matters.

Where generative capability adds leverage — summarizing storm logs, drafting narrative sections of regulatory submissions, translating protection settings documents into structured records — it operates with retrieval grounding against your estate and provenance stamps on every claim. No agent issues a switching order, submits a market bid or grants remote access; those boundaries are architectural. The agentic layer's output is reviewed work moving faster, with the audit trail to prove it — which is the difference between automation that survives a CIP audit and automation that becomes the finding.

Integration engineering: the estate stays, the contracts change

Grid estates are layered over decades — EMS from one vendor, ADMS from another, MDM inherited through mergers, condition monitors bolted on as projects — and the fastest way to stall a program is to treat that as debt to repay before starting. Dataq Software wraps the estate instead: adapters at native cadence, normalizers for vendor formats, schema contracts that make each upstream system answerable for freshness and shape. Control systems are read-first by policy; nothing writes into a controlled system without your management-of-change process, and most builds never need to.

The vendor chain gets the same treatment. Condition-monitoring vendors, weather services, DER aggregators, meter-data processors and market interfaces all become contract parties to the data plane: formats specified, freshness SLAs enforced, test harnesses run per delivery. When a vendor changes a format, the contract test fails before the decision layer notices — which is the difference between an integration estate and an integration apology. Aggregator telemetry gets the strictest contracts of all, because dispatching on unverified aggregation is how flexibility programs lose their credibility.

  • Read-first control integration

    every path into EMS, ADMS or substation automation starts read-only at native cadence; write access is designed only where a workflow demands it and executed through your MOC process with your engineers in the loop

  • Aggregator verification contracts

    DER aggregations verified against meter and telemetry evidence per event, with confidence scoring and dispatch blocks when verification lapses — flexibility markets run on verified megawatts, not promises

  • MDM and settlement bridges

    validation-edit-estimation rules surfaced as explainable artifacts, settlement interfaces contract-tested per market cycle, so disputes resolve against a shared record rather than dueling exports

  • Contract-tested weather and price feeds

    forecast providers, price curves and registry interfaces behind freshness SLAs and golden-day replay tests, because a stale weather feed is a silent forecast killer

How an engagement runs

The engagement shape is deliberately short on ceremony and long on measurement. It opens with a two-week diagnostic on the workflow you nominate — not a maturity assessment of the whole estate — and the diagnostic's product is a baseline in your own telemetry, a named delta with the confounders listed, and a scoped proposal only if the delta justifies the build. You keep the measurement design either way.

  • Weeks 1-2: diagnostic and baseline

    workflow instrumentation, leak quantification, contract inventory, feasibility read with the delta estimate and the confounders named in writing

  • Weeks 3-8: first vertical slice

    one lane of the workflow live behind flags with replay and evidence running from day one — one control zone's forecast, one transformer fleet's condition ledger

  • Weeks 9-16: gates, controls, expansion

    controls landed, quality gates live, remaining zones and fleets added, capacity and decision telemetry reporting to the operations review

  • Quarterly: challenger reviews

    forecasting methods, dispatch policies and restoration heuristics re-evaluated in the registry, baselines re-verified, the roadmap re-priced against measured deltas

The team and the operating rhythm

A grid build team is small and senior: a lead engineer who has carried operational or protection responsibility, data engineers fluent in protocol semantics and meter data, an optimization specialist who treats solver behavior as an empirical question, and a delivery lead who has carried an operations pager during storm mode. The team sits with control-room supervisors, asset engineers and the settlement desk — not in a program tower — because the context that makes the difference lives in the switching log, the DGA trend and the conversation before a window is committed.

The rhythm is the same at every client: a weekly operations review on the metric, a monthly delta review against the locked baseline with finance in the room, and quarterly challenger sessions where forecasting methods, dispatch policies and prioritization models defend their traffic. Runbooks, evaluation suites and the decision-replay store transfer to your teams as gate conditions during expansion, so the program's dependency on us shrinks on a schedule rather than by negotiation.

Failure modes we design against

Programs in this sector fail in known ways, and most of the failure modes are organizational wearing a technical costume. We name them here because prevention is a design decision, not a cultural aspiration.

  • Pilot purgatory

    a demo built on extracted data that never integrates — prevented by shipping the first vertical slice through your change process, flags and gates included, within the first two months

  • Model drift from the network

    forecasts and studies running against a network model the field has quietly outgrown — the network-model spine reconciles field edits continuously or the build is not done

  • Dispatching unverified flexibility

    DER aggregations assumed rather than verified — telemetry confidence is a dispatch gate, and the block fires before the regulator asks why the megawatts never arrived

  • CIP evidence debt

    audit preparation consuming a team every cycle because artifacts were manual — the evidence layer generates them continuously or the build is not done

  • Control-room rejection

    a dashboard the operators ignore because it contradicts the alarm philosophy or hides its provenance — operator co-design and decision replay are how trust gets built

  • Metric gaming

    SAIDI improving on paper while complaints rise — reliability metrics are weather-normalized and audited against complaint and call records, not self-reported from the OMS

Questions grid operations leaders ask us

  • Do you touch the EMS or protection systems?

    We read. Write paths to any controlled system go through your MOC process with your engineers in the loop, and most builds never need them. Recommendations land in operator workflows; the control room keeps its authority.

  • Can this work with a partial AMI rollout?

    Yes — the fabric starts with what exists, and the diagnostic names where sensing investment pays. Forecasts and studies degrade honestly where coverage is thin, and the coverage map becomes a capital argument.

  • How do you handle vendor-locked SCADA and MDM exports?

    Adapters and normalizers are part of the build. Your licenses stay; the export contracts change so decisions can consume the data. Every vendor interface ships with schema contracts and freshness SLAs.

  • What happens to our operators and dispatchers?

    Their judgment moves up a level: the build assembles context and drafts options; humans approve, sequence and own the risk decisions. Programs are justified in decisions per shift, not headcount removed.

  • How does this survive a NERC CIP audit?

    Every artifact ships with lineage, scope boundaries and approval gates. The audit reads the build as another managed system — and the evidence layer makes that read fast because the records are already structured.

  • Can you price against the delta rather than the effort?

    Yes, where the metric is clean. Forecast error, imbalance cost, reliability indices and evidence effort all baseline in your telemetry during the diagnostic; the feasibility read names the confounders first.

  • What is the smallest program worth running?

    One control zone's forecast, one transformer fleet's condition ledger, or one settlement dispute stream — whichever has the cleanest baseline. If the diagnostic cannot measure the delta in two weeks, we say so and stop.

  • Who owns the models after you leave?

    Your teams, with runbooks, model documentation, evaluation suites and the decision-replay store transferred as gate conditions during expansion. Several programs run entirely in-house after the second quarter.
