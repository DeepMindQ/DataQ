Care Management & Population Health

Inside a health plan, one member month crosses every machine: an eligibility file sets the coverage, a claim arrives as an 837 and meets the adjudication edits, an authorization request pends behind missing clinical evidence, a care manager dials a call list, a quality engine computes a gap, a risk-adjustment coder reviews a chart, and a member waits. The plan's margin and its star rating are decided in the same decision machinery — how precisely risk is stratified, how completely authorization evidence assembles, how accurately codes reflect charts, how reliably outreach reaches the member who needs it. This page is the engineering picture of that machinery as Dataq Software sees it from inside payer estates — where it leaks, and the six deep builds that close it.

How the plan runs, eligibility to star rating

The transaction spine starts at enrollment: an 834 sets the member's coverage, the eligibility files flow to providers, and the claims arrive as 837s through the EDI gateway. Adjudication engines run the edits — benefit design, coding conflicts, coordination of benefits, network status — and release the 835 remittances that providers reconcile. Payment integrity screens around the core: pre-pay edits, clinical validation for high-cost claims, post-pay recovery and FWA screening feeding an SIU queue. Every transaction has a clock and an exception path, and the exception paths are where plans actually spend their staffing.

The clinical layer runs across the same spine. Utilization management and prior authorization review requests against criteria — InterQual- and MCG-class policy sets — with clinical evidence assembled by hand from faxes, portals and chart requests. Care management platforms stratify members, mostly on claims history, and run outreach programs whose engagement economics go unmeasured. Quality engines compute HEDIS measures on the schedule NCQA defines; star ratings turn those measures into bonus economics; and risk adjustment extracts HCCs from medical records through coder review, with RADV audits looming over every unreconciled code.

Around all of it run the obligations: CMS program audits, state Medicaid rules, NCQA measurement standards, HIPAA privacy, and the interoperability duties CMS-0057-F added — payer-to-payer exchange, provider access, prior-authorization API timelines. Medicaid redeterminations add an eligibility churn problem that moves members in and out of coverage on state schedules. The estate is transactional, deadline-driven and siloed — which is exactly why the leaks below persist: they live in the seams between the adjudication engine, the care platforms and the quality estate nobody owns end to end.

Where the plan leaks margin and stars

Ask plan operations leaders where the damage concentrates and the answers converge on the same eight places. Dataq Software's diagnostic scores each of them in your own telemetry before recommending a build, because the leak that dominates a Medicare Advantage plan is different from the one that dominates a Medicaid managed-care contract — and the fix is engineering either way.

  • Risk stratification on claims lag

    Claims arrive with a 30-to-90-day lag, so the member the care program sees is the member of last quarter. Social-determinants signals, engagement data and clinical context sit in separate estates; rising-risk members are found by the hospital instead of by the plan. The targeting gap is measurable in program engagement rates against matched controls.

  • Prior auth queues that pend on missing evidence

    Requests pend because the clinical evidence exists in a chart nobody has assembled, criteria are applied inconsistently across reviewers, and the 278 status is invisible to the provider who is waiting. Turnaround is measured in days for determinations that need hours; provider abrasion compounds with every re-keyed fax.

  • Payment integrity screening blind

    Pre-pay edits false-positive heavily, so provider abrasion eats the savings; post-pay recovery chases claims that were recoverable pre-pay; FWA screening runs on rules tuned years ago. The economics per program — false-positive rate, recovery yield, provider complaint volume — are unknown because the screens and the outcomes live in different systems.

  • HEDIS and stars assembled under deadline

    Measure runs happen near the NCQA calendar with manual evidence assembly, so the gap-closure interventions that would have moved stars never ran. Provider-facing quality reporting lags the measurement year by enough that the providers it could coach have already re-signed. Star economics make this the most expensive reporting ritual in the plan.

  • Risk adjustment evidence thin for RADV

    Coder review samples untargeted, chart retrieval slow, and the connection between a coded HCC and its supporting documentation reconstructed only when the audit arrives. Unsupported codes are discovered by the auditor rather than by the plan's own validation — the most expensive possible way to learn them.

  • Outreach operations on call lists

    Care outreach, scheduling and follow-up run on exported lists with no feedback loop: attempts, connections, outcomes and program enrollment live in different systems. Members fall through the gaps between contact attempts, and the engagement economics — lift per outreach dollar — go unmeasured because the targeting and the outcomes never meet.

  • Appeals and grievance clocks at risk

    Denials generate appeals with strict clocks; evidence assembly is manual; determination letters are drafted by hand; and the root causes that generated the appeal — an edit, a criteria misread, a coding conflict — persist upstream. Overturn rates by reason code are the diagnostic nobody assembles routinely.

  • Redetermination churn unmanaged

    Medicaid redeterminations move members on state schedules, and the plan discovers the churn in the 834 file after it happens. Proactive renewal support, coverage-gap tracking and the transition of care-management programs across eligibility changes run on manual lists. Every silent loss is a member whose care the plan was coordinating yesterday.

The pattern across all eight leaks: the plan makes its decisions on yesterday's data with today's staff, and the regulators now audit the evidence trail as closely as the outcomes. Context is a data engineering problem before it is a modeling problem — which is why the first build is almost always the member decision lakehouse.

The builds: six deep engineering services for payers

The catalog below is the whole of our payer and care-management practice. Each build is named for the workflow it changes, sized in the diagnostic, and shipped with the controls the workflow's regulator will expect. None of them require replacing the adjudication platform or the care-management system; all of them require taking the data contracts and the audit trails seriously.

1. Prior authorization and UM automation

The build maintains a criteria library mapped to computable expressions, ingests requests through portals, EDI 278 and provider APIs, and assembles the clinical evidence automatically from claims, care records and chart requests. Administrative criteria resolve automatically with their decision trail; clinical criteria route to a nurse reviewer with the evidence pre-collated and the rationale pre-drafted for confirmation. Determination letters generate from the record with the required content, and 278 status flows back to the requesting provider in real time instead of by phone.

Clock telemetry reports turnaround per case, per service line and per provider contract, and the pended queue becomes a managed instrument rather than a mystery. Auto-approval exists only for lanes your UM committee explicitly certifies; everything else carries a clinical signature. The measurable deltas are turnaround time, first-pass determination rate and provider abrasion — in the published record, a national health insurer cut prior-authorization turnaround 62% with clinical evidence assembled automatically, and the same instrumentation measures your baseline before anyone touches the queue.

2. Claims intelligence and payment integrity screening

The build attacks the false-positive problem first: edit libraries tuned against paid-after-review outcomes, duplicate and COB detection on the transaction stream, clinical validation targeted to the claim patterns that historically yield recovery, and an SIU workbench that pre-assembles investigation files from claims history, provider behavior and network data. Every screen outcome feeds back into threshold review, so the screens tighten instead of flooding providers with rework.

The economics are tracked per program: false-positive rate, recovery yield, provider complaint volume and downstream dispute rate — reported from the same store, so the integrity portfolio is managed on yield rather than on caught-claim counts. FWA screening adds the graph layer: provider behavior networks, billing-pattern anomalies and referral loops scored with human-in-the-loop case assembly. The measurable deltas are pre-pay savings at a lower abrasion rate, recovery cycle time and the SIU queue's cleared-cases-per-analyst.

3. Risk adjustment coding integrity and RADV evidence

The build targets coder review where it matters: a chart-review prioritization model over member-year candidates that weights audit exposure, coding-pattern anomalies and documentation availability, so the sample follows risk rather than rotation. Reviewers receive pre-assembled evidence — the chart excerpts, problem lists, claims history and prior coding — with suggested HCCs traceable to their source text, and every coder decision logs its evidence for the audit trail RADV validation demands.

The evidence layer is the point: every HCC on the submission carries its documentation trail continuously, unsupported-code rates surface through the plan's own validation samples rather than through the auditor's, and RADV audit responses assemble from the store instead of from a retrieval scramble. Provider education closes the loop: coding-gap patterns route back to the practices that generate them. The measurable deltas are validation-sample throughput, unsupported-code rate at the plan's own checkpoints and audit-response effort.

4. Quality measure engine: HEDIS and star economics

The build turns the measure calendar into an always-on pipeline: HEDIS-class measures computed continuously on the event fabric — claims, pharmacy, laboratory and chart-review data — with hybrid measures' chart retrieval orchestrated ahead of the deadline instead of inside it. Gap lists generate for care managers and provider practices with the evidence attached, and closure interventions route to the outreach orchestration build with their outcomes feeding back into the same store.

The economics layer makes stars a managed instrument: measure-by-measure trajectory against the rating bands, the value of a point on the specific measures the plan can move, and the provider-facing reporting that shows a practice its own gaps in time to act. Every measure computation is versioned and replayable, so the NCQA submission and the internal dashboard cannot disagree. The measurable deltas are gap-closure rate on targeted measures, chart-retrieval cycle time and the star trajectory reported from the pipeline rather than projected after the fact.

5. Care management targeting and outreach orchestration

The build replaces claims-lag stratification with a member state model: claims, clinical data, pharmacy utilization, social-determinants signals and engagement history joined into one governed substrate, refreshed as events land. Rising-risk identification runs against that state with evidence behind every flag; program matching scores clinical need and engagement likelihood together instead of assigning members by blunt rules; and the care-management platform receives its targeting as an API rather than as a monthly file.

Outreach orchestration executes the contact plan across channels with follow-up policy: attempts, connections, outcomes and program enrollment tracked per member, so the engagement economics — lift per outreach dollar, contact-channel effectiveness, program completion rates — report from the same store as the targeting. Holdout groups measure the programs honestly. The measurable deltas are engagement lift on contacted cohorts, program enrollment throughput and the rising-risk members the plan now reaches before the admission rather than after it.

6. Member, appeals and grievance operations

The build industrializes the member-facing clocks: appeals and grievances intake with structured reason capture, evidence assembly from the same member substrate the other builds read, determination letters generated from the record with the required content, and deadline tracking with escalation gates so no clock is discovered late. Root-cause routing sends the patterns that generate appeals — an edit, a criteria misread, a coverage miscommunication — back to the workflow that created them.

Member communication grounds in the member's own records: authorization status, benefit questions and program enrollment answered from the governed substrate with citations, so the call center and the portal stop contradicting each other. Redetermination support rides the same layer — proactive renewal outreach, coverage-gap tracking and care-program continuity across eligibility changes. The measurable deltas are appeal cycle time, overturn rates by reason code, member-experience scores on communication and the churn the plan now sees coming.

The data plane: from batch feeds to a member event fabric

Every decision gap in a plan traces back to a data gap, and most data gaps trace back to the same habit: the adjudication engine, the care platforms and the quality estate each hold a fragment of the member, and the joins happen in monthly files with no lineage. The data plane build inverts the pattern where it matters. Claims, eligibility, authorization status, care events and pharmacy data land through change-data-capture into a governed lakehouse in near real time; the core administration keeps its validated rhythm, but the decisions above it stop depending on last month's extract.

Governance is engineered, not asserted. Every feed carries a schema contract with a named owner, a freshness SLA and a reconciliation check against the source system's own controls. Terminology binding is enforced at the contract layer — ICD-10, CPT, HCC categories, NDC — so a measure, a code and a cohort mean the same thing in the quality meeting and the audit. Member identity is conformed with survivorship rules across enrollment changes, consent and communication preferences travel with the record, and minimum-necessary access is enforced at retrieval.

  • Transaction-first ingestion

    claims, eligibility and authorization status captured as streams into the lakehouse without touching the adjudication platform's batch jobs, so UM, integrity and quality decisions run on current state

  • Clinical and SDOH joins

    care records, pharmacy events and social-determinants signals normalized to the same contract layer as claims, so stratification reads the member rather than the claim history

  • Terminology-bound semantics

    ICD-10, CPT, HCC and NDC bindings enforced at the contract layer, so measures, coding and integrity screens stay consistent across teams and audit cycles

  • Minimum-necessary enforced at retrieval

    PHI access governed at query time with purpose binding visible in the replay store, so an access audit is a report the system produces, not a project

The decision layer: scores with audit and member tails

Payer operations run on decisions with tails on both sides: a determination letter carries timeliness and medical-necessity duty, an edit rejection carries provider-abrasion consequences, a risk-adjustment code carries RADV exposure, a stratification flag decides who gets a care manager. The decision layer treats those tails as first-class requirements. Every decision records its inputs, feature values, model versions, criteria and reason codes in a replay store that answers audit, appeal and RADV questions as queries. When a reviewer asks why, the answer is retrieved, not reconstructed.

Champion/challenger governance is built in rather than bolted on: challengers run on shadow traffic with performance tracked in the registry the model-governance function owns, promotions happen through the gates your governance process already defines, and rollbacks are rehearsed rather than improvised. The same decision service layer hosts authorization scoring, edit tuning, stratification and program matching, so the governance surface is one registry, one replay store, one set of gates — instead of every algorithm carrying its own audit story.

The agentic layer: file assembly without judgment authority

The backlog work in payer operations — authorization packets, appeal files, chart retrieval for coders, measure evidence collection, investigation preparation — follows patterns an agentic workforce can absorb without touching judgment authority. Dataq Software's agent platform runs digital workers with explicit role scopes: the tools they may call, the systems they may read, the actions that require a human checkpoint and the budget of work per run. Every run logs its steps and outputs to the same evidence store the human workflow uses, so an agent's file is auditable exactly like an analyst's.

Where generative agents add leverage — summarizing case files, drafting determination rationales and appeal responses, pre-coding chart excerpts for coder review — the pattern is grounded generation with citation obligations and human authorship of record. Agents never issue determinations, never change a code, never send member or provider communication without review; they move the work to the decision point and stop. Capacity is measured in cleared cases per FTE, which is how the operations leaders who sponsor these programs justify them upward.

Integration engineering: the core administration stays, the contracts change

Payer estates are transactional, deadline-bound and change-averse by design, and the fastest way to fail a program is to promise greenfield. Our integration posture is contractual: we define schema contracts with each system owner, land data through the EDI gateway and governed APIs, write back through the interfaces your change control already governs, and keep every new component independently deployable behind feature flags. The adjudication platform and the care-management system are not replaced; they are supplied with better context and drained of the decision work they were never good at.

The vendor chain gets the same treatment. Criteria libraries, edit vendors, SDOH data providers and engagement platforms sit behind an abstraction layer with contract tests, so a vendor swap is a configuration change with a migration runbook rather than a re-platform. Where a legacy interface must stay, the offload pattern applies: the existing interface keeps running while decision surfaces read from the governed stream the offload produces. This is unglamorous work and it is the reason the builds above hold up in production instead of in a pilot.

  • Write-back through transaction interfaces

    determination records, edit outcomes and outreach updates flow through the interfaces your change control already governs, with no direct database writes anywhere

  • Vendor abstraction with contract tests

    criteria, edit, SDOH and engagement vendors behind one interface so a vendor swap is configuration with a runbook, not surgery on the workflow

  • CMS-0057-F exchange as an engineering lane

    payer-to-payer and provider-access APIs built on the same contract layer, so the interoperability duty and the decision substrate reinforce each other

  • Feature-flagged, independently deployable

    every build ships behind flags with rehearsed rollback, because the change advisory board and the compliance function will ask exactly that question

How an engagement runs

The engagement shape is deliberately short on ceremony and long on measurement. It starts with the two-week diagnostic: one workflow baselined end to end, the leak quantified in your own telemetry, the interfaces and contracts inventoried, and a written feasibility read you can circulate — including the uncomfortable findings. If the delta is real, the build is scoped against the gates your change, compliance and model-governance processes define; if it is not, you keep the measurement design and the baseline either way.

  • Weeks 1-2: diagnostic and baseline

    workflow instrumentation, leak quantification, interface inventory, feasibility read with the delta estimate and the confounders named

  • Weeks 3-8: first vertical slice

    one lane live behind flags with replay and evidence running from day one — for authorization that is one service line; for integrity, one edit family

  • Weeks 9-16: gates, controls, expansion

    governance review landed, remaining lanes added, capacity telemetry reporting to the operations and compliance reviews

  • Quarterly: challenger reviews

    model and threshold challengers evaluated in the registry, criteria and edit libraries re-tuned, the baseline re-verified against holdouts

Delivery runs in your delivery rhythm, not a parallel one: our engineers work in your repositories or a shared workspace with the same CI, the same security review and the same access model your teams use. Knowledge transfer is part of the gates, not a closing slide — by the expansion phase your engineers are shipping changes to the builds, because the runbooks, contracts and evaluation suites are theirs from week one.

The team and the operating rhythm

A payer build team is small and senior: a lead engineer who has run decision systems in health plans, data engineers who own the transaction and contract layer, ML engineers who own scoring and evaluation, a controls engineer who owns the evidence layer, and domain leads who have run UM review, payment integrity, quality measurement or care operations in production plans. A compliance liaison joins for anything touching CMS or NCQA scope, and agentic-workflow specialists join for the file-assembly builds. The team works to your release calendar and your gate vocabulary from the first week.

The rhythm is the same at every client: weekly operations review on the workflow's own telemetry, fortnightly gate submissions to change and compliance governance, quarterly challenger reviews, and an incident practice that treats the decision store and the evidence layer as production-critical systems — because by the second quarter, they are. Escalations go to named engineers, not to a queue.

Failure modes we design against

Payer programs fail in known ways, and most of the failure modes are organizational rather than mathematical. We name them in the feasibility read and design against them explicitly, because a program that cannot survive its second quarter — or its first program audit — is not worth starting.

  • Pilot purgatory

    a demo built on extracted data that never integrates with the adjudication platform — we prevent it by shipping the first vertical slice through your change process, flags and gates included

  • Model-risk stall

    the scoring service stuck in review because documentation was an afterthought — the registry, evaluation results and lineage land with the build, not after it

  • Abrasion blowback

    integrity screens that trade savings for provider revolt — false-positive rate and dispute volume are first-class metrics, and threshold review runs on paid-after-review truth

  • Gap-closure gaming

    measure rates moving while member outcomes stand still — closure actions are audited against the underlying services actually delivered, with holdouts as the honest check

  • Stratification staleness

    care targeting running on last quarter's claims because the clinical feeds never landed — contract-enforced freshness is a launch condition, not an optimization

  • Evidence debt

    program audit preparation consuming a team every cycle — the evidence layer generates artifacts continuously or the build is not done

Questions payer leaders ask us

  • Do we have to replace the core administration platform?

    No. The builds consume transaction streams from your adjudication platform, EDI gateway and care systems, and write back through the interfaces your change process governs. The core stays; it stops being the bottleneck for decisions and evidence.

  • How does this survive a CMS program audit?

    The audit artifacts are generated continuously: UM determination trails, coding evidence, measure computation lineage and API performance records. The response pack assembles from the store, and the plan's own validation finds unsupported codes before the auditor does.

  • Will providers accept tighter automation?

    Providers feel the difference as speed and consistency: evidence assembled before the reviewer sees the case, status visible in real time, fewer false-positive rejections. Abrasion metrics are first-class, and threshold review runs on paid-after-review truth.

  • Who signs determinations and codes?

    Qualified humans, always. Evidence assembly and rationale drafting are automated; the clinical determination and the coding decision remain human acts with their own accountability, and every override feeds the threshold review.

  • Can this respect HIPAA and state privacy law?

    Yes. Minimum-necessary access is enforced at retrieval, consent and communication preferences travel with the record, and the stack has been delivered under US regulatory regimes. Privacy duties are build requirements with their own evidence, not assumptions.

  • How do you measure success without gaming?

    Baselines are locked during the diagnostic, holdouts are used where experiments are cheap — care outreach is one of the few places in a plan where a clean experiment is routine — and every metric reports from system telemetry rather than a status deck.

  • What is the smallest program worth running?

    One workflow, one baseline, one vertical slice — prior authorization and payment integrity are the common starters because their clocks and outcome data are already instrumented. If the diagnostic cannot find the delta, we say so and stop.

  • Who owns the build after you leave?

    Your teams, with the runbooks, contracts, evaluation suites and registry entries transferred as gate conditions during expansion — not as a closing deliverable. Several programs run entirely in-house after the second quarter.

Bring one workflow — authorization, payment integrity, quality measures, risk adjustment or care outreach — and we will baseline it in your own telemetry within two weeks, then tell you plainly whether the delta justifies the build. The opening call costs an hour; the feasibility read stays with you either way.
