Fashion & Luxury

In fashion and luxury, the product line is a portfolio of bets placed a season ahead of demand. The buy commits capital against style, colorway and size grids long before a customer decides; allocation decides which door gets which pieces; markdown decides what the mistakes cost; and the brand promise decides what the customer will forgive. The margin lives or dies in the decision machinery underneath: how honestly the size curve was modeled, how fast a hero-SKU shortage triggers a transfer, how rigorously returns and counterfeits are handled. This page is the engineering picture of that machinery — the systems it runs on, the eight places it leaks, and the six deep builds Dataq Software ships to fix them.

How a seasonal business actually runs

The year is a collection calendar, and everything hangs off it. Design and merchandising finalize the range in PLM: styles, colorways, size grids, fabric and trim choices, bills of materials and costing. Sourcing commits production against fabric lead times, minimum order quantities and the nearshoring-versus-offshore trade-off — speed against margin, reaction against cost. The buy is placed by market and door cluster before any customer has voted with a purchase, which is why the size-curve decision is the largest single bet in the business.

Once product lands, the distribution machinery divides it across tiers: own retail and e-commerce, wholesale doors fed by EDI, concessions inside department stores, and marketplaces. Allocation assigns pieces to doors against expected curves; replenishment tops up the sizes that sell; and the transfer engine moves stock between doors when a hero SKU starves in one boutique while gathering dust in another. RFID reads, POS transactions and e-commerce orders stream into the inventory ledger; the availability promise in the app is only as honest as that ledger.

Then the season does what seasons do: some lines fly, most sell, some sit. Markdown decisions cascade through the calendar, constrained by brand rules that forbid discounting in some tiers. Returns flow back through BORIS processing with fraud screening and grading; resale and authentication programs extend product life; clienteling associates carry the relationship in apps and memory; brand-protection teams chase gray-market listings and counterfeits. The daily texture — allocation proposals, transfer requests, markdown ladders, return dispositions — is where the season's margin is actually decided.

Where the margin leaks

Ask merchandising and retail directors where the money leaks and the answers converge on the same eight places. Dataq Software's diagnostic scores each in your estate before recommending a build, because the dominant leak in a fashion group is rarely the dominant leak in a luxury maison.

  • The size-curve bet placed blind

    Curves are inherited from last season's history rather than modeled by style, market and door cluster, so every buy bakes last year's errors into this year's commitments. The correction arrives as markdown, which is why end-of-season discounting absorbs forecast error instead of the buy fixing it.

  • Hero-SKU starvation across doors

    The piece that carries the season sells out in one boutique while sitting overstocked in another, and transfers wait for a weekly review. Availability on the pieces that define the brand is decided by allocation rules of thumb, not by demand transfer logic.

  • Markdown depth as an apology

    Markdown timing and depth follow calendar habit rather than elasticity by market, so profitable stock is discounted early and dead stock lingers too late. Full-price sell-through — the metric the industry actually lives on — is nobody's daily number.

  • Returns economics invisible

    BORIS processing grades and disposes by manual rules; fraud patterns adapt faster than the checks; and the true cost of a return — freight, grading, refurbishment, resale loss — is never attached to the style that generated it. Bracketing is priced into nothing.

  • Clienteling runs on associate memory

    The best associates carry client preferences in their heads and their own notebooks; the app shows a transaction list. Consent state is disconnected from profiles, so the personalization the maison can legally execute is thinner than what its staff already knows.

  • RFID truth unverified

    Tagging programs produce read events nobody reconciles to cycle counts and availability promises. The app promises a size the floor cannot find, and the error surfaces as a cancelled order rather than a correction.

  • Gray market and counterfeits unmanaged

    Unauthorized sellers and fake listings multiply across marketplaces faster than manual takedowns; diverted stock is indistinguishable from authorized flow because authentication records are not attached to items. Brand equity erodes quietly while legal chases cases one at a time.

  • Seasonal peaks concentrate the risk

    Collection launches, holiday trading and sale events concentrate demand into windows the fulfillment network was never load-tested for. Cost per order at the ninety-fifth percentile is unknown, and the promise the app makes during the peak is a hope, not an engineering number.

The pattern across all eight: the seasonal bet is made once, in the buy, and every later decision — allocation, transfer, markdown, returns — either protects it or amortizes it as loss. That is why curve intelligence comes before markdown automation: fix the bet, then manage the residue.

The builds: six deep engineering services for fashion and luxury

The catalog below is the whole of our fashion and luxury practice. Each build is named for the workflow it changes, sized in the diagnostic, and shipped with the brand rules and privacy constraints the workflow expects. None of them require replacing the PLM or the POS; all of them require taking the data contracts and the brand codes seriously.

1. Assortment and size-curve intelligence

The curve build replaces inherited history with modeled demand. Size curves are estimated by style family, market and door cluster from sell-through joined to returns, traffic and lost-demand signals; the buy plan consumes those curves as constraints, with scenario views that show margin and markdown exposure before the commitment is signed. Demand transfer between styles — the customer who wanted the colorway in another size — is modeled so the curve reflects substitution rather than pretending every miss was independent.

The same intelligence prices the pre-season conversation with sourcing: fabric and trim lead times, minimum order quantities and the nearshoring-versus-offshore trade-off are evaluated against the curve's confidence, so a fast-react second order is planned where the model says demand is uncertain rather than everywhere. Curve accuracy is tracked season over season, and the planners who adjust the model see their value added measured. The measurable deltas are curve accuracy by family, buy coverage on the confident cells, and full-price sell-through.

2. Allocation, replenishment and transfer for seasonal assortments

The allocation build assigns the buy door by door against the curve, respecting floor capacity, presentation minimums and brand-tier rules. Replenishment for carryover and core lines runs on demand sensing rather than fixed rules; the transfer engine watches hero-SKU velocity across doors and proposes moves the moment a boutique starves while a sister store gathers overstock, with logistics cost and presentation impact priced into every proposal. Size-set optimization runs at the same grain, because a curve that is right for the market can still be wrong for the door.

Proposals land in a planner-approved lane: the allocation team sees the reasoning, overrides anything, and every override feeds the threshold review. Wholesale doors ride the same logic through the 852 sell-out feed — markdown support and replenishment proposals to wholesale accounts are computed on their actuals rather than on negotiated hope. Hero-SKU availability is reported daily by door cluster, because it is the metric the season lives on.

3. Markdown and full-price sell-through optimization

The markdown build replaces calendar habit with elasticity by market, style and channel. Markdown ladders are simulated before they are published — projected sell-through, cannibalization into full-price, brand-rule constraints on discounting in luxury tiers — and the ladder executes automatically within the guardrails the brand sets, with every step logged to the replay store for the post-season review. Price-image rules protect the tiers where discounting damages the brand, and the guardrails are the brand team's, not the algorithm's.

The objective is explicit: maximize realized margin across the season, not minimize leftover units at any cost. Full-price sell-through becomes a daily operational number with an owner, markdown depth becomes a decision rather than a ritual, and the post-season review compares each ladder's promise to its outcome on the same basis. Where brand strategy deliberately overrides the model — protecting price integrity on a hero line — the override is recorded and its cost measured, so next season's conversation starts from evidence.

4. Clienteling copilot under luxury service codes

The clienteling build gives associates one governed view of the client: purchase history across channels and brands, size and fit records, consent state, preferences the client has stated, and the maison's service codes for how that information may be used. The copilot drafts outreach and appointment suggestions grounded in those records, with citations to the client's own history, and hands anything transactional to governed APIs or to a human. It never speaks for the associate; it removes the swivel-chair and the memory tax.

Consent architecture is the foundation, not a wrapper: profiling rules are documented per purpose, the lawful basis is enforced at execution time, and every outreach decision is replayable if the client or a data-protection authority asks. Associates keep authorship of the relationship; the platform keeps the evidence. The measurable deltas are repeat-purchase rate, appointment conversion and the share of top-client relationships actively managed rather than dormant — measured against holdout cohorts where the client base allows.

5. RFID inventory truth and availability assurance

The RFID build turns item-level tagging into an operational control system. Read rates are monitored by item type and read point; cycle counts trigger by anomaly rather than by calendar; and the stock ledger reconciles RFID reads, POS transactions and e-commerce orders continuously, so the availability promise the app makes reflects what the floor can actually fulfill. Hardware gaps are assessed door by door and closed where the loss history justifies the investment, because tagging without read integrity is a cost with no return.

Availability assurance closes the loop: phantom stock is corrected at the source, pick failures in store fulfillment are traced to read-point or process causes, and the accuracy metric the brand reports is computed from reconciled evidence rather than from a sampling exercise. In luxury, where the item is the brand, item-level truth also feeds authentication and resale — the same tag that guarantees the floor count guarantees the provenance story.

6. Returns, resale and authentication economics

The returns build prices and polices the BORIS stream. Return authorization applies policy consistently across channels; fraud scoring combines behavior, item and payment signals to separate bracketing from abuse; and grading plus disposition rules route items to resale, refurbishment or destruction with the economics of each path computed rather than assumed. The cost of a return — freight, grading, refurbishment, resale loss — is attached to the style, size and channel that generated it, feeding the curve model and the size guidance upstream.

Resale and authentication extend the same fabric: digital product passports and authentication records attach to items at birth, marketplace listings are verified against them, and brand-protection analytics monitor gray-market and counterfeit activity with takedown evidence assembled automatically. The resale channel becomes a margin line with its own economics rather than a brand-risk afterthought — and the authentication layer is what makes it safe to operate.

The data plane: item-level truth, season by season

Every decision gap in fashion and luxury traces to a data gap, and most trace to the same habit: item-level truth exists in fragments — RFID reads here, POS transactions there, wholesale sell-out weeks late in EDI — and the merchandising systems were never fed all of it. The data plane build lands item-level events, transactions, returns and wholesale feeds through governed ingestion into one lakehouse, with the style-colorway-size grid from PLM as the spine every stream joins to.

Governance is engineered, not asserted. Every feed carries a schema contract with a named owner, a freshness SLA and a reconciliation check against the source's own controls. Client identity is resolved once across brands and channels, with consent state attached at the profile rather than rediscovered per campaign. Every downstream consumer — curve models, allocation, markdown, clienteling — reads through the contract layer, so a brand's data change fails loudly at the contract instead of silently in a season.

  • Item-level event ingestion

    RFID reads, POS transactions and e-commerce orders landed with exactly-once semantics so the stock ledger reconciles in near real time

  • Grid-first joins

    every stream keyed to the PLM style-colorway-size grid with pack and prepack expansion, so curve history survives assortment changes

  • Wholesale sell-out normalization

    852 flows and account portals normalized to one grain, so markdown support and replenishment for wholesale run on actuals

  • Consent-attached client master

    client identity resolved across brands with consent state carried on the profile, so profiling questions are answered by query, not reconstruction

The decision layer: curves, guardrails and brand codes

Fashion runs on decisions with brand tails: an allocation carries the promise of the collection, a markdown carries price integrity, an outreach carries consent duties. The decision layer treats those tails as first-class requirements. Every decision records its inputs, feature values, model versions, brand rules and reason codes in a replay store that answers audit questions as queries — which curve version, which elasticity estimate, which service code produced this ladder, this transfer, this suggestion.

Champion/challenger governance is built in: challengers run on shadow seasons where volume allows, curve and markdown models are tracked in one registry merchandising and brand governance share, and rollbacks are rehearsed. The same decision service layer hosts allocation, markdown, replenishment and returns disposition, so the governance surface is one registry and one replay store instead of four tools with four audit stories. Brand codes are encoded as constraints the optimizer cannot violate, which is what makes automation safe in a luxury house.

The agentic layer: digital workers behind the atelier

The backlog work in fashion — transfer proposal drafting, returns grading, brand-protection takedown evidence, client file preparation, markdown ladder review — follows patterns an agentic workforce absorbs without touching judgment authority. Agents run with explicit role scopes: the systems they may read, the tools they may call, the actions that require a human checkpoint, and a budget of work per run. Every run logs its steps and outputs to the same evidence store the human workflow uses.

Where generative agents add leverage — drafting takedown notices from listing evidence, summarizing client histories before an appointment, assembling post-season buy reviews — the pattern is grounded generation with citation obligations and a human author of record. Agents never publish markdowns without the guardrail lane, never contact clients directly, and never alter authentication records; they move the work to the decision point and stop. Capacity is measured in cleared cases and prepared appointments per associate.

Integration engineering: the estate stays, the contracts change

Fashion estates are layered across brands, seasons and channels, and the fastest way to fail a program is to promise a unified-commerce replacement. Our integration posture is contractual: schema contracts with each system owner, data landed through governed ingestion, write-back through the allocation, POS and e-commerce interfaces your change process already controls, and every new component independently deployable behind feature flags. The PLM is not replaced; it becomes the spine every stream joins to.

The vendor and partner boundary gets the same discipline. Image recognition, RFID hardware, marketplace integrations and authentication vendors sit behind abstraction layers with contract tests, so a vendor swap is configuration with a runbook rather than a re-platform. Wholesale accounts connect through the same crosswalk layer, so 852 sell-out and portal data reconcile to the grid. This is unglamorous work and it is the reason the builds hold up in production instead of in a pilot.

  • Write-back through your interfaces

    allocation proposals, markdown ladders and transfer moves flow through the planning and POS interfaces your change process already governs

  • Vendor abstraction with contract tests

    RFID, image recognition and marketplace vendors behind one interface, so a swap is configuration rather than surgery

  • Season-calendar release discipline

    decision changes ship outside trading peaks with rehearsed rollback, because a failed markdown publish during sale is not an outage but a brand event

  • Feature-flagged and independently deployable

    every build ships behind flags with brand-tier-specific guardrails, because the maison's rules differ from the diffusion line's

How an engagement runs

The engagement shape respects the collection calendar, because that calendar is the operating rhythm of the business. It starts with the two-week diagnostic: one workflow baselined end to end — a category's curves, a door cluster's allocation, a market's markdowns, a return stream's economics — the leak quantified in your own data, the contracts inventoried, and a written feasibility read you can circulate, including the uncomfortable findings. If the delta is real, the build is scoped to land before the season that needs it.

  • Weeks 1-2: diagnostic and baseline

    workflow instrumentation, leak quantification, feed inventory, feasibility read with the delta estimate and confounders named

  • Weeks 3-8: first vertical slice

    one lane live behind flags with replay and evidence from day one — for allocation that is one door cluster; for returns one channel

  • Weeks 9-16: gates, controls, expansion

    brand-rule guardrails landed, measurement controls live, remaining lanes added, availability and sell-through telemetry reporting to the merchandising review

  • Quarterly: challenger reviews

    curve and markdown challengers evaluated against locked season baselines, thresholds re-tuned, the post-season review regenerated from the live store

Delivery runs in your merchandising rhythm, not a parallel one: our engineers work in your repositories or a shared workspace with the same CI, security review and access model your teams use. Knowledge transfer is part of the gates, not a closing slide — by expansion, your planners and engineers are shipping changes to the builds, because the runbooks, contracts and evaluation suites are theirs from week one.

The team and the operating rhythm

A fashion build team is small and senior: a lead engineer who has run merchandising decision systems, data engineers who own the item-level spine and contract layer, ML engineers who own curve, markdown and fraud models, a controls engineer who owns the evidence layer, and a domain lead who has run allocation or retail merchandising in a real estate. Brand-experience specialists join for clienteling builds, because the service codes are requirements the engineering must respect. The team works to your collection calendar and gate vocabulary from the first week.

The rhythm follows the collection calendar: weekly operations review on the workflow's own telemetry, fortnightly gate submissions timed between trading peaks, quarterly challenger reviews aligned to season closes, and an incident practice that treats the decision store and evidence layer as production-critical systems — because by the second quarter they are. Escalations go to named engineers, not to a queue.

Failure modes we design against

Programs like these fail in known ways, and most failure modes are organizational rather than mathematical. We name them in the feasibility read and design against them explicitly, because a program that cannot survive its second quarter is not worth starting.

  • Season-blind rollout

    a decision service landed mid-season without rehearsal — releases are scheduled against the collection calendar with rollback drills before every peak

  • Curve model capture

    the model optimizes last season instead of current demand — curves are back-tested against locked baselines and challenger-reviewed every season

  • Clienteling resistance

    associates ignore the copilot because it feels like surveillance — profiling is designed with the service codes, and the associate keeps authorship of the relationship

  • Markdown whiplash

    ladders published faster than stores can execute — execution telemetry is part of the decision loop, and the ladder respects reprice cycles

  • Consent retrofit

    clienteling automation shipped before the lawful basis was engineered — consent architecture precedes the first automated outreach

  • Evidence debt

    brand and privacy reviews consuming a team every quarter because artifacts were manual — the evidence layer generates them continuously or the build is not done

Allocation by door: consignment logic and sell-through evidence

Luxury distribution is negotiated door by door: concession terms, consignment versus outright, and sell-through thresholds that decide whether a style renews for the next season. The data behind those negotiations — door-level receipts, weekly sell-through by SKU, hold-back clauses, return rights at season end — usually lives in the wholesale team's spreadsheets while the ERP carries only shipment truth. When a door underperforms, the renewal argument is anecdotal; when a style overperforms, the reorder decision waits for a report that arrives after the buying window closes. The gap is not analytics talent, it is the missing contract-level data model that joins terms to movement.

The build is a door-economics ledger: terms metadata (consignment flag, markdown rights, exclusivity windows) attached to every door, sell-through computed weekly against season pace targets, reorder and transfer proposals respecting concession law and brand adjacencies, and end-of-season returns priced against contractual rights before the goods move. Dataq Software keeps merchandisers in the loop — proposals land in planner-approved lanes with reasoning attached — and measures the program on one number: revenue per door per season against the pace plan the buying team signed. If the ledger does not move that number, the honest read is that the constraint sits elsewhere, and we say so.

Questions fashion and luxury leaders ask us

  • Will this override our merchandisers?

    No. Proposals land in planner-approved lanes with the reasoning shown, and every override feeds the threshold review. The allocator keeps authority; the model removes the arithmetic and the guesswork.

  • Luxury service cannot be automated.

    Agreed, and the builds are designed that way: the copilot prepares, grounds and cites; the associate decides and relates. Automation handles evidence assembly and arithmetic; it never speaks to your client for you.

  • Do we need full RFID coverage first?

    No. The availability build starts where read rates already work and expands by item type and door. Cycle-count triggers and ledger reconciliation deliver value at partial coverage.

  • How do you protect price integrity?

    Brand rules are encoded as constraints the markdown optimizer cannot violate, and deliberate overrides are recorded with their measured cost. Price integrity becomes an enforced policy rather than a hope.

  • Is resale worth the engineering?

    When authentication records attach to items at birth, resale becomes a measured margin line with its own economics. The diagnostic prices it against your gray-market exposure and return volumes before any build is scoped.

  • How does this pass a data-protection review?

    Consent state enforced at execution, profiling rules documented per purpose, decision replay for every automated outreach, and DSAR joins across brands as queries. The review becomes a walkthrough of evidence the system produces.

  • What is the smallest program worth running?

    One category's curves or one return stream — both have clean baselines and measurable deltas within a season. If the diagnostic cannot find the delta, we say so and stop.

  • Who owns the build after you leave?

    Your teams, with runbooks, contracts, evaluation suites and governance artifacts transferred as gate conditions during expansion. Several programs run entirely in-house after the second season.

Pick one season, one category or one door network: we baseline sell-through, allocation and markdown performance in two weeks and price the delta honestly. If the numbers do not support a build, you keep the baseline and the measurement design, and we part as friends.
