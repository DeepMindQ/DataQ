Maritime, Ports & Shipping

A container terminal is a physics scheduling problem wrapped in EDI and labor rules: every berth window, gantry cycle and truck appointment competes for the same finite quay, yard and gate capacity, and the schedule only holds when all three agree. Dataq Software builds the decision layer that sits beside your terminal operating system — berth and yard what-ifs, gate-flattening analytics, demurrage evidence automation — reading the Navis N4 or OPUS estate first and saying plainly when the numbers do not support a build. The TOS runs the moves; we build the layer that decides where the next move should go.

Terminal operating systems run the moves; they do not decide the trade-offs. Navis N4 and OPUS Terminal will execute a berth plan, dispatch a yard crane and record a gate transaction with precision, but the judgment about which vessel slips one tide, which block gets housekeeping tonight and which appointments to flatten lives in spreadsheets, in a planner's memory and in a dispute file nobody reconciles until the demurrage invoice lands. That gap between execution and decision is where moves per crane hour, truck turn times and free-time clocks are won or lost, and it is where every serious terminal improvement program actually starts.

Dataq Software works in that gap. We build TOS-adjacent decision services — read-first against your existing estate, never proposing to replace the TOS that runs your quay — plus EDI quality surveillance across the 301, 304, 315 and 322 message flows that bind your plan to carrier reality. Every engagement starts with a two-week baseline measured against your own data: moves per crane hour by shift, re-handle rates by block, gate turn-time distributions by hour, demurrage dispute outcomes by evidence class. If the delta is not real, we say so and stop, because a decision layer built on an untested assumption is just another dashboard nobody opens.

The loudest symptom is rarely the deepest cause. Demurrage disputes look like billing problems; most trace to gate-in timestamps, free-time clock start events and 322 message gaps, which makes them a data-contract problem before they are a billing problem. Yard congestion looks like a labor problem; it usually traces to berth variance one or two tides earlier. Gate peak looks like a trucking problem; it is usually appointment adherence that nobody measured. We follow the operational chain from quay to gate to invoice, because that is the chain your money actually travels.

Berth windows, vessel rotation, and the schedule nobody trusts

Berth scheduling is negotiation under physics. Ocean carriers publish rotations months ahead, alliances shuffle loops when a string slips, and the terminal must commit quay cranes, gangs and yard space long before anyone knows the actual arrival. A berth window that looks stable on paper collapses when two 400-meter vessels land inside the same tide window and the yard cannot absorb four thousand import lifts in twelve hours. The plan is only as good as its assumption about what the yard can hold, and that assumption is usually a number carried over from last year.

The decision layer we build treats the berth plan as a probabilistic contract: vessel ETAs from 301 schedules and historical call behavior, yard occupancy projected from actual TOS state, labor availability from the gang board, all combined so the planner sees the collision two days out instead of two hours out. The what-if service answers the questions planners already ask each other — what happens to crane productivity and re-handles if the 06:00 berthing slips to 14:00, which export block releases if the feeder holds one window, what this rotation does to Friday's rail cut — with the arithmetic attached to every answer.

Crane productivity: moves per crane hour is an output, not a lever

Moves per crane hour is the terminal's headline number, and it is almost entirely an output of decisions made elsewhere. A crane rated for thirty cycles loses productivity to twin-lift mismatches, to hatch coverage planned in the wrong order, to yard blocks too dense to double-stack, to yard cranes queuing on the transfer lanes. Gantry cycles, spreader lock times and trolley path all show up in the TOS event stream; the distance between the thirty-five moves per hour the customer expects and the twenty-four the quay delivers is usually visible in that stream before it is visible on the quay.

We do not sell crane optimization as a black box. We instrument the leak points above, baseline them over two weeks against your own moves, and show which ones carry real money in your operation. On a two-berth terminal running three thousand lifts a day, one move per crane hour of recovered productivity is worth more than most software budgets — but only if the leak is in sequencing and deployment rather than in the steel. If the constraint is an older crane with a drive problem, the analysis will say that, and you should spend the money on the drive.

Yard planning: blocks, streets, and the re-handle tax

Every container placed badly is picked twice. Re-handle minimization is a planning discipline: keeping export blocks sequenced by vessel and port of discharge so load runs clean, segregating dangerous goods per the IMDG tables, holding reefers on plug rows with monitoring coverage, and leaving streets wide enough for twin-lift carriers to pass. Housekeeping moves are the tax paid for yesterday's compression — shifting containers between blocks at night, when quay demand is low and labor is expensive, to open the density tomorrow's vessels will need. Block and street management decides tomorrow's crane productivity this afternoon, which is why yard planners live under the berth plan's shadow.

The yard what-if service we build answers one question continuously: given tonight's occupancy by block, tomorrow's vessel work plans and the rail load plan, which housekeeping moves are worth their labor cost, and which blocks will choke first? It reads TOS yard state, planned stowage and train compositions, then projects density hour by hour so the night shift receives a housekeeping list ordered by tomorrow's actual need rather than by whichever block looked crowded at the sixteen-hundred meeting. The same projection prices the alternative: hold the housekeeping, accept the re-handles, and see the crane-hour cost of that choice before committing.

Yard congestion is usually berth variance lagging by one tide. Terminals that tighten berth-window adherence see re-handle rates fall without touching the yard itself, and the connection stays invisible unless berth and yard data are analyzed together.

Gate operations: turn times, OCR exceptions, and peak-hour flattening

The gate is where terminal physics meets highway physics. Truck turn time decomposes into queue time, kiosk or OCR lane time, problem-resolution time at the trouble desk and yard transaction time, and each component has a different owner. Gate OCR reads license plates, container numbers, chassis numbers and seal checks; its exception rate, not its raw throughput, is the metric that matters, because every misread container number becomes a trouble-desk stop that quadruples that truck's dwell and poisons the 322 gate-in message your invoicing depends on. Appointment and booking systems hold the other half of the story: adherence data nobody reads daily.

  • Appointment adherence analytics

    Measure booking-system compliance by carrier and hour: who books and no-shows, who books three windows and uses one, what share of peak arrivals held appointments at all. Flattening starts with adherence data the appointment system already has but nobody reads daily.

  • Peak-hour flattening model

    Move discretionary traffic — empties, exports, empty-for-export — out of the morning hump into the early-afternoon valley, simulated against yard and labor capacity before you publish new gate hours to the drayage community.

  • OCR exception triage

    Classify misreads by root cause: plate damage, container number wear, chassis mismatch, seal discrepancy. Fix the top two causes and the trouble-desk queue and the 322 message gap shrink together.

  • Turn-time evidence for carriers

    Publish turn-time distributions by hour and lane to shipping lines and drayage providers, so the monthly conversation runs on data instead of anecdote — the same evidence-pack discipline we apply to demurrage disputes.

None of this requires new gate hardware. The appointment system, the OCR lanes and the TOS already generate every record these analytics need; the engineering work is joining them reliably and putting the answer in front of the gate manager before the morning hump, not in a report after it. The same join feeds billing: when gate-in timestamps, appointment records and the 322 flow agree, free-time clocks start on defensible evidence, and the demurrage problem below shrinks before anyone negotiates.

The EDI layer: where the terminal's plan meets the carrier's reality

Terminals do not run on documents; they run on message flows. UN/EDIFACT and ANSI X12 messages carry the vessel schedule in the 301, export bookings and rail service tenders in the 304 family, ocean shipment status milestones in the 315, and terminal gate activity in the 322, with CODECO movement reports and COPRAR loading orders keeping container-level truth aligned between parties. When a 315 reports loaded where no 322 gate-in exists, when a 301 slot fix lands after the yard planned the block, when 304 rail tenders drift from the load plan, commercial data and operations data diverge — and every downstream system inherits the divergence.

Dataq Software's EDI quality surveillance sits beside your existing gateway — the MIGs, the VAN links, the in-house translators — and reconciles message truth against TOS truth continuously: counts, timestamps, reference integrity, voyage alignment. The output is a daily exception list an analyst can actually clear, plus trend evidence for the quarter when a carrier's flow degrades and the conversation needs numbers instead of complaints. We never propose replacing your gateway; we make the message spine accountable to the operation it serves.

Demurrage and detention: a data-contract problem before a billing problem

Demurrage is a clock problem before it is a money problem. The free-time clock starts on an event — discharge, availability, last container lifted — and every dispute begins as an argument about that event: when it happened, whether the terminal's system recorded it, whether the 322 message or the EDIFACT movement report corroborates it. Detention runs on a different clock with different parties and the same evidentiary problem. When clocks are reconstructed from incomplete messages, invoices go out on contested evidence, and the dispute cycle consumes more analyst hours than the disputed revenue is worth.

The evidence pack, built as automation

The evidence pack is the unit of resolution. A complete pack for one container dispute contains the discharge timestamp and its source system, the gate-out attempt records, the appointment history, the 315 status chain, the free-time terms from the tariff in force on that date, and the arithmetic from clock start to clock stop. Built by hand, that is an afternoon per container; at a few thousand disputed containers a month it is a department. Built as automation, it is a service that assembles the pack from your existing systems, checks the arithmetic, and hands only the genuinely contested cases to a human.

Invoice audit before dispute

Most demurrage and detention revenue leakage is internal before it is external: free-time applied from an outdated tariff, clock starts on the wrong event type, weekend and holiday rules misapplied against the tariff's own calendar, duplicate container billing across voyages. We audit a trailing window of invoiced containers against the underlying events, quantify the error classes in dollars, and then automate the pre-invoice check so the errors stop at generation. Shrinking disputes at the source costs less than staffing them downstream, and the audit itself becomes the business case for the automation.

Terminals rarely know their true dispute win rate because evidence is assembled after positions have hardened. Automating the evidence pack inverts that: the data decides, and the negotiator arrives with the pack instead of an opinion.

Compliance operations: VGM, dangerous goods, and reefer setpoints

Compliance work is data work with regulatory deadlines. Verified gross mass under SOLAS Chapter VI means no VGM, no load, so the export flow needs VGM status per container before yard planning commits it, plus the method and evidence trail when an auditor or a carrier asks. Dangerous goods under the IMDG Code require declarations checked against segregation tables, placarding data carried into the yard plan, and emergency contact information that survives EDI round-trips. Reefer containers carry setpoints, ventilation settings and genset requirements that must persist from gate-in through load, with temperature excursion records whenever anything drifts.

  • VGM surveillance

    Track VGM presence, timeliness and source per booking and per shipper, and surface containers heading toward load without acceptable VGM while there is still time to weigh or correct. The audit trail the regulation expects is retained as a by-product.

  • DG declaration checks

    Validate IMDG declaration fields against segregation tables and yard placement, flagging conflicts before the container lands in a block it would have to be moved out of at night.

  • Reefer excursion evidence

    Correlate setpoint, actual temperature, plug events and genset swaps into per-container timelines, so the conversation about a spoiled load starts from shared data instead of a screenshot of a temperature log.

  • Inspection readiness

    Keep compliance artifacts — VGM methods, DG declarations, pre-trip inspection records — queryable per container and voyage, because the audit question is always about one container, never about the average.

Empty containers, chassis, and the street-turn nobody books

Empty container repositioning and equipment imbalance are a slow leak in terminal and carrier economics. Import-heavy terminals fill with empties that must be trucked to depots at everyone's expense, while export-driven yards beg empties from the same depots days later, and chassis pools absorb the difference in split fees and street-turn disputes. The street-turn — matching an available empty at the gate directly to an export booking that would otherwise fetch one from a depot — is the classic fix, and it fails for data reasons: the terminal knows what empties it holds, the carrier knows what it needs, the drayage provider knows what it is hauling, and nobody's systems talk at the moment the match is possible.

The street-turn matcher we build watches gate-out events, empty inventory and export booking demand in near real time, proposes matches with the carrier's equipment control rules applied, and tracks realized against proposed matches so the program's value is measured rather than asserted. Chassis adds a parallel problem: pool utilization, split configurations and the fee structure that makes a street turn cheap or expensive for each party. Both are matching problems over data that already exists in the TOS, the carriers' EDI and the pool's systems; the decision layer just has to join them at the moment the match can still be executed.

Labor planning: gangs, shifts, and the vessel work plan

Labor is the terminal's largest controllable cost and its most rule-bound. Longshore agreements fix shift structures, gang sizes, overtime triggers and guarantee payments; the vessel work plan fixes how many gangs each ship needs and when. The planner's problem is that vessel ETAs move, gang call deadlines are contractual, and a called-but-idle gang is real money with no productivity attached. When berth plans are optimistic and labor plans are built on them, idle-gang cost is the invoice for that optimism, paid shift after shift.

The labor decision layer joins the berth forecast, the vessel work plans, the gang board and the agreement's rules into one forward view: expected gang-hours by shift, the probability that a call will be wasted against realistic arrivals, and the trade-off between guarantee payments and crane-hour delay. We build it read-first against the TOS dispatch records and the labor system's own history, because the rules live in your agreements. Our job is the arithmetic and the visibility; renegotiating the agreement is not our lane and never will be.

Hinterland flows: rail load plans, barge windows, and drayage appointments

A terminal is an interchange, and its worst congestion often arrives by train. The rail load plan commits export containers to trains with cut-offs the yard must honor; when 304 rail tenders disagree with the load plan, or import availability lags the train's planned pickup, containers either miss the train or the yard burns re-handles recovering them. Barge calls add windows and per-call lift commitments; drayage appointments scale the gate's peak problem to the whole hinterland. Each mode runs on its own clock, and the yard is the buffer that absorbs their disagreements — until the buffer fills.

The decision services here are forecast-and-commit: import availability forecasts against train cut-offs, rail load plan feasibility checked against live yard density, barge window planning against crane availability, and drayage appointment capacity that respects what the yard can actually transact per hour. The data lives in the TOS, the rail operators' EDI, the barge operators' calls and the truck appointment system. The value is in joining them early enough that the plan changes before the train is built, not after.

Port call optimization: just-in-time arrival and berthing windows

Just-in-Time arrival asks vessels to slow steam so they arrive inside a berthing window the terminal can actually honor — burning less fuel, arriving to a berth that is ready, cutting anchorage queues and the pilot-and-tug churn that goes with them. It only works when the terminal's berth forecast is trustworthy and shareable: no carrier will slow steam against a berth plan that revisions hourly. Port community systems and national single windows provide the exchange rails for port call data, and customs interoperability rides the same rails. The terminal's job is to make its own window forecast good enough to be worth exchanging.

That is a data engineering problem before it is a collaboration problem: arrival predictions that blend AIS, 301 schedules and your own historical pilot, tug and mooring performance; berth window commitments issued with confidence levels attached; and a feedback loop that measures how often the window held. Terminals that cannot forecast their own berth windows cannot participate in port call optimization, and the fix is internal before it is ecosystem. The two-week baseline tells you which part of the forecast is the weak link.

Schedule reliability and alliance network effects

When a string slips, it slips along an alliance loop: the same eight vessels rotating through the same ports, each terminal inheriting the previous port's variance. Carrier schedule reliability is therefore a terminal planning input, not a marketing number — a terminal that plans Friday against published ETAs without modeling that loop's historical variance will over-book labor on good weeks and bleed idle-gang money on bad ones. Network effects mean one port's recovery decision, an extra shift or a compressed berth window, ripples into the next port's yard three days later as a density problem nobody ordered.

We model loop variance from your own call history: for each service string, the distribution of call deviations, the covariance between consecutive calls, and what that implies for berth and labor plans two weeks out. The 301 schedules and the actual berths recorded in your TOS already contain the model; it has simply never been assembled. Where a loop is too young or too irregular for prediction, the honest output is a wider labor plan with explicit probabilities instead of false precision.

EU ETS and FuelEU: CO2 reporting starts at the quay

For European terminals and their carrier customers, emissions reporting has become an operational data requirement. EU ETS maritime extends carbon cost to port calls, and FuelEU Maritime sets greenhouse-gas intensity limits on the energy vessels take on board; both generate reporting obligations that trace to quay-side events — shore power connections, at-berth hours, genset runs, bunker deliveries. A terminal that cannot produce per-call energy and time data will be reporting by estimate, and estimates are where compliance cost and audit risk concentrate.

The reporting layer we build collects the underlying signals — shore power meter readings, reefer and genset consumption, equipment energy where instrumented, berth occupancy times from the TOS — and assembles them into per-call artifacts aligned to what the carrier's EU ETS and FuelEU filings actually need. It is the same pattern as every other service on this page: the data already exists in your estate, and nobody has joined it into the artifact the regulation asks for. The join is the engineering work.

Equipment health: spreaders, drive systems, and predictive signals

A quay crane that drops out mid-shift costs more than its repair: the vessel work plan slips, gangs stand idle, and the berth window erodes into the next vessel's slot. Crane and yard equipment — positioners, spreaders, hoist and gantry drive systems, anti-collision and snag detection — emit fault codes and cycle data continuously through PLCs and the maintenance system. The predictive question is whether cycle-time drift, fault-code clustering and event frequency lead failures reliably enough to schedule intervention before the shift is lost. Sometimes they do; sometimes the fleet is too small or the sensors too sparse for honest prediction, and we will say so.

  • Cycle-time drift tracking

    Baseline hoist, trolley and gantry cycle times per crane and watch the drift by direction and load class. It is the earliest cheap signal most fleets already produce, and it needs no new sensors.

  • Fault-code clustering

    Group CMMS work orders and PLC fault codes into families so recurring spreader and positioner failures surface as a pattern instead of a ticket list nobody reads.

  • Downtime priced in lost moves

    Join equipment downtime to vessel work plans and berth windows so each failure class is priced in lost crane moves — the number maintenance budgets are actually argued against.

  • Feasibility gate

    Two weeks of data tells us whether your signal density supports prediction or only early warning. We scope accordingly instead of selling a model your fleet cannot feed.

ISPS security operations: the data behind the facility security plan

The ISPS Code obliges port facilities to run a port facility security plan — access control, restricted areas, security levels, drill and incident records, declaration of security procedures. In practice the plan is a document and its execution is a data trail: gate access logs, OCR plate records, seal and scan events, security-level change logs, incident reports. When the recognized security organization audits, or when an incident triggers review, the question is whether that trail can be produced per event and per period. Producing it by hand from scattered systems is exactly the kind of work that fails under audit pressure.

We treat PFSP execution as evidence engineering: join the access and gate systems' records into per-period artifacts the facility can produce on demand, watch the logs for the anomalies that matter — after-hours access to restricted areas, seal discrepancies at gate-in, repeated access refusals at the same lane — and keep drill and incident records queryable. No new security hardware is required; the trail already exists in the systems the plan already mandates. The deliverable is a security file you can open on demand instead of a scramble.

Reference architecture: TOS-adjacent decision services

The architecture follows one rule: the TOS remains the system of record for every move, and the decision layer reads it without ever rewriting it. Operational sources land in a quay-to-gate event lake, quality surveillance reconciles message truth against operational truth, and the decision services consume both. Their outputs return to the business through the channels planners and carriers already use — the port community system, billing evidence, labor boards and dashboards. Each service is scoped by a two-week baseline against your own data before anyone commits to build it.

Questions port, terminal, and carrier leaders ask us

  • Will you touch our Navis N4 configuration?

    No. We read through APIs, event exports and logs, and every service we build runs beside the TOS. Where a decision service needs a write-back — an appointment confirmation, a housekeeping task — it goes through your existing interfaces and your team approves the change.

  • Can arrival prediction really support labor planning?

    Sometimes. Where a service loop has enough call history, prediction against the published ETA is useful; where it does not, the honest answer is a wider labor plan with explicit probabilities. The two-week baseline tells you which case you are in.

  • We already have BI dashboards. What is different?

    Dashboards report what happened; decision services answer what to do before it happens, with the trade-off arithmetic attached. We usually build on top of existing BI investments rather than replacing them.

  • Who owns the demurrage evidence packs?

    You do. The automation runs against your systems, packs file to your document store, and your billing team decides what to send. We build and maintain the machinery; the commercial judgment stays yours.

  • How do you handle union and labor-rule complexity?

    The rules live in your agreements, and we encode them as explicit, auditable logic — never as a black box. Planners see why a recommendation was made, which rule produced it, and what an override costs.

  • What if the baseline shows we do not need this?

    Then we stop. That outcome is precisely why the two-week baseline comes first: it is cheaper for both sides than a project built on an assumption nobody tested.

Bring one vessel rotation, one month of gate transactions and one demurrage dispute file to a two-week baseline: we will measure moves per crane hour, re-handle rates, turn-time components and dispute evidence gaps against your own data, and put a number on the delta. You keep the baseline either way. If the physics, the labor rules and the message flows support a decision layer, Dataq Software scopes and builds it read-first beside your TOS; if they do not, we tell you what would have to change first, and we stop there.
