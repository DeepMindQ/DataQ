Warehousing & Fulfillment

Fulfillment margins die in unmeasured seconds: the travel step nobody times, the re-handle nobody counts, the pack station that idles between waves. Dataq Software builds the labor-and-flow analytics layer over your existing WMS — Manhattan, Blue Yonder, Körber or warehouse-native — read-first, with engineered-standard calibration, returns disposition economics and peak-readiness simulations. Where the numbers say the constraint is steel, layout or a rate contract, we say so and stop.

Fulfillment margins are not lost on the rate card; they are lost in seconds the operation never measures. The pick path that runs forty feet longer per order, the put-wall that starves for fifteen minutes after a late wave, the pack station balanced for yesterday's unit density — none of these appear on a standard WMS report, because the WMS executes work; it does not judge the flow that work produces. Between order release and carrier pickup sits a chain of small timing decisions that decides whether the building makes money on the day, and most buildings cannot see that chain end to end.

Dataq Software builds for that chain: a labor-and-flow analytics layer that reads your WMS, your labor management system, the conveyor and sorter PLC logs and the RF scan stream, and turns them into decision services — slotting what-ifs, wave-pressure management, engineered-standard calibration, returns disposition economics and peak-readiness simulation. Read-first means we instrument what exists before proposing anything new, because the estate already records most of what we need. The two-week baseline prices the gap: productivity per hour by function, station idle time, re-handle counts, cut-off attainment. If the delta is not real, we say so and stop.

Peak season is when the layer earns its keep, but it is built in the quiet months. Staffing curves, slotting re-tunes for promo SKUs, wave templates and dock windows are all decided weeks before the first peak order drops. The building that simulates those decisions against its own historical data walks into peak with a dated chokepoint list; the building that does not walks in with hope and a spreadsheet of last year's totals. Both buildings have the same data; only one has joined it.

Order profiles: units, cases, and eaches rewrite the building's math

Nothing changes a warehouse's economics faster than its order profile shifting. A building slotted for case picking that receives two years of each-driven e-commerce growth is a building fighting its own layout: pick faces sized for pallets serving carts meant for units, batch waves that made sense at forty lines per order dissolving at four, travel distance per unit doubling as velocity spreads across the catalog. The profile shift is visible in the WMS order data long before it is visible in the P&L — orders per wave, lines per order, units per line, units per line by zone.

The analytics layer tracks profile drift as a first-class metric: units versus cases versus eaches by day, by channel and by zone, with the slotting consequences each drift implies. When the each-share of a consumer-goods DC rises from a fifth of volume to a third across two seasons, the golden zones that made sense for cases are actively wrong for units, and the labor hours already show it before anyone re-slots. We quantify that cost per profile so the re-slot decision is priced rather than felt, and so the argument about pick methods stops being an argument about taste.

Profile drift also explains half the arguments between operations and the network team. The network plans on SKU-level forecasts; the building executes on bins, and the two meet at the pick face, where a velocity shift the network sees as a forecast adjustment shows up as a pick face that cannot hold a day of supply. We keep the join explicit: profile by channel and zone on one side, pick-face capacity and replenishment frequency on the other, so a drifting SKU is flagged for slotting review before it becomes a replenishment emergency on the shift board. The conversation changes from whose forecast was wrong to which pick face moves this week.

Slotting: velocity, cube, and the golden zone that stopped being golden

Slotting is the highest-leverage static decision in the building: velocity-based placement puts fast movers into golden zones nearest dispatch, cube and weight affinity keeps dense items low and on pick faces their cartons actually fit, and affinity grouping keeps commonly co-ordered items adjacent for batch and cluster routes. Every WMS estate carries the data slotting needs — item velocity, cube, weight, order affinity — and most buildings re-slot too rarely, because the re-slot's labor cost is visible this month while its pick-path savings are diffuse next quarter. That asymmetry is why slotting debt accumulates quietly and then surfaces as overtime.

The slotting service we build runs as a continuous what-if rather than an annual project: it reads actual pick data from the WMS and RF scans, simulates re-slot candidates against engineered standards, and produces a priced list — which moves, into which slots, at what labor cost, returning what pick-path and replenishment savings. Promo and seasonal SKUs get a separate lane, because a promo SKU slotted into a golden zone on Monday is a congestion problem by Wednesday; re-tuning for the promo calendar is a different decision from the annual re-slot, with a different clock and a revert date.

A golden zone is a claim on congestion as well as on distance. Buildings that re-slot on distance alone tend to move congestion into the same aisles the peak plan is about to load, which is why slotting and peak planning belong in one model.

Wave release: the two-minute decision that sets the night

Wave planning is the building's daily scheduling act: which orders release, in what sequence, with what replenishment pre-work, so that pick, pack and ship hit the carrier pickup windows without starving any station. A wave released too large floods the floor and queues the pack stations; too small and sortation and trailer loading idle between waves; released in the wrong order and the same-day cutoff orders sit behind next-day stock. Waveless release trades the batch structure for continuous flow, which suits goods-to-person floors and turns the release decision into a rate-control problem instead of a batch-sizing problem.

Wave-pressure analytics make the release decision measurable: orders outstanding by cutoff class against pick and pack rates per hour, replenishment demand against pick-face inventory, and projected carrier-cutoff attainment for each release option. The dashboards run from WMS release data and the labor system's actuals. The decisions they support are concrete: change the wave template, re-order the cutoff sequence, move one large customer's orders into the fourteen-hundred wave so the sixteen-hundred sortation runs level, or hold the wave ten minutes because the dock is not ready. Same-day SLAs are won or lost in exactly these calls.

Wave-pressure analytics also close the loop after the fact: cutoff attainment by carrier window, late-wave origins traced back to upstream steps, and the wave template's actual behavior recorded next to its plan. That history is what makes next quarter's template changes evidence-based, and it is the same data the peak simulation consumes when the season plan is built. Buildings that keep this loop running rarely argue about wave templates in the abstract; they change one variable, measure the night, and keep what worked.

Pick methods and the travel-time ledger

Pick method is a per-zone design decision, not a philosophy. Batch picking amortizes travel across orders; cluster picking carries multiple order carts on one pass; zone picking trades travel for handoffs and balancing risk; goods-to-person eliminates travel entirely and replaces it with station throughput and queue design. The right method for a zone changes as the order profile shifts, which is why the decision needs a ledger: what travel actually costs per pick in each zone today, and what each candidate method would change it to.

  • Travel-time measurement

    Reconstruct actual pick-path time per order, per zone and per method from RF scan timestamps and task records. It is the baseline every method argument needs before it is an argument at all.

  • Method fit analysis

    Score each zone's profile — lines per order, units per pick, velocity spread — against batch, cluster, zone and goods-to-person economics using your own engineered standards, not vendor templates.

  • Task interleaving policy

    Decide when replenishment, cycle counts and put-away interleave with picking, measured against pick-path disruption and station starvation rather than against a policy inherited from a previous building.

  • Goods-to-person readiness

    For AS/RS grids and AMR floors, measure robot-to-human pick rates and station queue depth, and state plainly whether the profile justifies the automation's cost per unit.

The travel-time ledger usually surprises both sides of the argument. Zones that look efficient on paper carry hidden travel from congestion-blind slotting; zones that look expensive are often carrying replenishment travel booked to picking. Once the ledger exists, the method debate becomes arithmetic, and the two-week baseline is what builds it. It also feeds the LMS: travel is the largest component of most engineered standards, so a measured ledger recalibrates the standards themselves rather than the argument. Where RF scan gaps make the reconstruction unreliable, we say that too, because a ledger built on bad scan discipline is worse than none.

Automation floors: AS/RS grids, AMR fleets, and sorter controls

Automated and semi-automated floors change the bottleneck's location, not its existence. AutoStore-style AS/RS grids move the constraint to robot fleet sizing and port balancing; Locus- and 6River-style AMR fleets make pick rates a function of robot-to-human pairing and congestion on shared aisles; conveyor and sorter systems carry their own physics in read rates, merge gaps and recirculation loops. Cold-chain floors add an energy overlay to the same picture: door-open events, temperature excursions and defrost cycles compete with pick flow for the same hours. Every one of these systems emits telemetry that most buildings collect and almost none analyze as a whole.

The read-first posture matters more on automated floors than anywhere else in the building. The WES, the robot fleet APIs and the PLC event logs already produce the telemetry above; the engineering work is joining them into one flow model and finding the chokepoint by hour, which is usually not the subsystem the vendor conversation is about. Where the automation vendor's contract restricts data access, that restriction is itself a finding, and it goes in the baseline report with the cost of leaving it in place.

Labor management: engineered standards and the productivity argument

A labor management system stands on engineered standards — ELS values per function and per item characteristic, applied to actual work to produce expected versus actual productivity per hour. Where standards are calibrated, incentive pay is defensible and coaching is factual; where they have drifted from the building's current profile, the same system manufactures grievances, because incentive disputes are usually standard-accuracy disputes wearing payroll clothes. Manhattan and Blue Yonder LMS deployments carry the framework; the standards' accuracy is a living calibration problem, not a go-live artifact.

The calibration service reads RF task records, scan timestamps and item attributes, re-derives the actual time distributions per function, and quantifies where standards over- or under-state the work — by zone, by shift, by item class. The output is a calibration proposal both the operation and the workforce can inspect: which standards move, by how much, and what that does to incentive outcomes. Where the building runs no LMS at all, the same analysis establishes whether engineered standards would pay for themselves — sometimes they would not, and the report says so in the first paragraph.

Incentive pay disputes

Disputes concentrate where standards meet profile shifts: a standard built on case picking applied to each picking, a golden zone invalidated by a re-slot, a peak-temp population paid against standards calibrated on veterans. We produce the evidence file per dispute class — task records, standard values, measured time distributions — so the conversation happens on data and the standard changes once instead of being argued every month. The same evidence keeps the incentive system defensible for the workforce that beats it, which matters more than the savings.

Inventory accuracy: cycle counts, blind counts, and bin-level variance

Inventory accuracy is a program, not a number. Cycle counting programs earn their keep by targeting: A-movers and error-prone slots counted weekly, low-velocity bins on rotation, and blind counts — where the counter sees the location but not the expected quantity — as the only counts that measure process instead of confirming a prompt. Bin-level variance analysis then turns counts into causes: variance by slot type, by item class, by shift, and by the put-versus-pick handoff, each pointing at a different broken process.

The decision layer here is small and unforgiving: count-program design against RF task history, variance cause tables built from WMS adjustment records, and the one discipline metric that matters — does the program find errors before the customer does. Where variance clusters, the fix is usually operational and cheap: a pick zone with a relief-shift gap, a put-away lane with a label problem, a slot type the count rotation skipped. The analytics layer's job is to make the cluster visible, price it, and verify the fix in the next count cycle.

Returns: RMA intake, disposition grading, and the restock-or-liquidation decision

Returns are a second business running through the same walls, with its own economics and usually a tenth of the instrumentation. RMA intake authorizes and routes; disposition grading decides each unit's path — restock as A-grade, refurbish, donate, liquidate — and the decision is economic, yet most buildings make it with defaults inherited from a policy nobody prices: restock velocity versus grading labor, liquidation recovery rates by category, seasonal demand for the category, and the carrying cost of a graded-but-unsold unit through the quarter.

The returns economics service reads RMA records, grading outcomes, restock events and liquidation recoveries, then prices each disposition path per category and checks whether grading rules match the economics. A category whose liquidation recovery runs at a fifth of retail while its restock rate runs at eighty percent is being graded too conservatively; a category whose restocked units cycle back as returns within a month is being graded too generously. Both patterns are visible in six weeks of your own transactions, which is the window the baseline uses.

When grading rules change

Grading rules are operational, so the service stops short of changing them unilaterally: it prices the alternatives, shows the disagreement cases, and lets the returns supervisor flip the rule with the arithmetic on screen. Every flip is logged with its expected and realized outcome, so the rulebook improves against evidence instead of anecdotes. Liquidation partners get cleaner lots as a side effect, because the units that should never have been graded restockable stop arriving at the dock with return labels already questioned. That is how a returns operation stops being a cost center nobody trusts and becomes a margin line with a defensible number.

Cartonization, dim weight, and the rate-shopping stack

Parcel cost is decided twice before the label prints: cartonization decides which box mix each order ships in, and rate shopping decides which carrier and service takes it. Dim-weight rules make box choice a cost decision — an over-boxed order pays dimensional weight on air, and an under-boxed order pays in damage and returns. Cartonization engines read item cube, fragility and carrier rules; their quality lands directly in the freight invoice, and their blind spots show up as the box-size distribution nobody reviews.

We audit the stack end to end: cartonization output against actual invoice lines, rate-shopping decisions against the rates that were available, dim-weight factors against carrier audits, and the carrier mix against service-level performance by lane. The savings are contract-side as often as algorithm-side, but the analytics to see them come from data the building already has — the manifest, the invoices and the cartonization log. Where the mix is wrong, the fix is usually a rule change rather than a system change, and the audit shows which.

Dim-weight audits deserve their own discipline because carriers run them continuously: invoice re-bills arrive weeks after shipment, and an operation that does not reconcile them is quietly funding the difference. We join the manifest to the freight invoice lines and the cartonization log, so every re-bill lands against the decision that produced it — the box the engine chose, the rate the shopper picked, the dim factor the carrier applied. Patterns surface fast: one SKU family over-boxed since a master-data change, one lane where the rate shopper misses a cheaper service, one pack station where a manual override has quietly become habit.

Inventory placement and network flows: forward DCs, cross-dock, supplier-direct

Placement decisions happen above the building: which SKU sits in which forward DC, what flows supplier-direct, what cross-docks on inbound instead of occupying reserve locations. Forward positioning buys delivery speed with inventory fragmentation — every forward unit is a unit that cannot serve another region — and the right policy is a per-SKU economics question: velocity by region, demand variance, cube per unit, and the transport cost of a stockout versus a transfer. The network model is only as good as the building-level costs it is fed.

Inside the four walls, cross-dock, supplier-direct and value-added flows change the labor math: cross-docked volume bypasses put-away and reserve but loads the dock schedule and the quality-check station; supplier-direct volume bypasses the building entirely but shifts compliance and packaging work upstream; kitting and value-added services add labor that is often billed but rarely costed at task level. The flow-analytics layer quantifies each flow's true labor and dock cost from WMS task data, so the network team's placement model runs on building-level truth instead of standard costs nobody has audited in years.

Peak planning: staffing curves, temp ramp, and the simulation habit

Peak is decided months early: seasonal staffing curves, temp-labor ramp schedules, slotting re-tunes for promo SKUs, wave-template changes and dock-window reservations. The buildings that run good peaks share one habit — they simulate. A peak-readiness simulation replays last season's orders, adjusted for this year's forecast, through the building's actual capacities: pick rates by zone, pack station throughput, sortation limits, dock windows and labor availability by ramp week. The output is a bottleneck list with dates on it, not a feeling about the season.

  • Seasonal staffing curve design

    Ramp plans built from last year's actual hourly throughput against this year's forecast, with the temp-labor training drag made explicit. A first-week temp picks at a fraction of veteran rates, and the curve should say so before the requisitions go out.

  • Promo slotting re-tune

    Move the SKUs the promo calendar will make fast into golden zones before the promo starts, price the re-slot labor against the pick-path savings, and schedule the revert with the same arithmetic.

  • Chokepoint-by-hour simulation

    Run the peak day hour by hour: where the queue forms, which station starves, which wave template breaks. Fix the template in October instead of discovering it in December.

  • Cutoff and carrier-window stress test

    Verify that same-day cutoffs hold at peak volume against carrier pickup windows and dock capacity, with the failing hour identified in advance rather than over the phone that night.

Peak plans fail at the handoffs: the hour the temp shift overlaps the veteran break, the wave that lands during the carrier's early window. Simulations that model handoffs explicitly catch what average-rate arithmetic misses.

Dock scheduling and live-load windows

The dock is where the building's clock meets the carrier's clock. Live-load windows commit doors and labor to a carrier's arrival; when inbound arrives unannounced or outbound misses its window, the cost lands as detention, reworked load plans and trailers held past cutoff. Dock scheduling discipline — appointment adherence, door assignment logic, load sequencing against the route — is measurable from the same WMS shipping data and gate logs the rest of the layer reads, which is why it belongs in the same model rather than in a separate dock spreadsheet.

The analytics are specific: appointment adherence by carrier, dock utilization by hour, live-load versus drop-and-hook economics per lane, and a load-plan quality metric — how often trailer loads match their planned sequence, which drives unload time at the destination. For 3PL operations, dock performance doubles as a billing artifact: detention and inside-handling charges need the same evidence-pack discipline a container terminal applies to demurrage, and the same automation pattern applies — reconstruct the clock from gate logs and task data, assemble the evidence, audit before invoicing.

Appointment adherence is also where a building learns which carriers are honest about their clocks. The same logs that produce detention evidence show which carriers book windows they cannot hit, which arrive during the peak hour they were asked to avoid, and which drop trailers without the stop the schedule expected. None of that requires confrontation; it requires the distribution by carrier and hour in the weekly review, and the effect on dock labor planning is immediate: doors staffed against arrivals that actually happen instead of arrivals that were booked.

Safety and ergonomics: OSHA reality on the floor

Safety performance and throughput performance share mechanics: rushed picks from late waves, congested aisles from congestion-blind slotting, forklift routes through pedestrian-dense zones. OSHA recordkeeping and ergonomics focus improve when joined to flow data: incident and near-miss locations against pick-path density by hour, forklift telemetry — speed, impacts, battery events — against aisle traffic, and station ergonomics scored against the hours each station runs at peak. The joined view turns the safety file from a compliance artifact into an operations input.

The analytics here are deliberately modest: incident heat maps by hour and zone, forklift telemetry exceptions such as speed events and impact counts by intersection, and a station-hours-per-risk view that says which pack station redesign buys the most exposure reduction. Safety data lives in scattered systems — EHS logs, telemetry portals, WMS task records — and joining it is the whole service. When the joined view contradicts the training calendar or the staffing plan, that contradiction is the deliverable, not a bug.

Cold-chain and food-grade buildings carry a variant of the same math: temperature-zone crossings add steps to every pick path, protective clothing changes engineered-standard values, and door discipline competes with flow for the same hours. The layer reads the same telemetry with the temperature overlay added, so the chokepoint-by-hour view shows which zone's door discipline is costing throughput, and the ergonomic view accounts for the standards difference between ambient and freezer work. None of this needs new sensors; it needs the temperature zones in the model instead of in a binder.

Throughput bottleneck diagnostics: chokepoints by hour

Every building has a chokepoint, and it moves. At six in the morning it is replenishment; at eleven it is pack stations; at fifteen it is sortation; on promo Mondays it is the put-wall. Station balancing and chokepoint identification by hour is the diagnostic that precedes every capital and labor argument on this page: before buying robots, know whether the constraint was ever picking; before adding pack stations, know whether the wave template starved the ones you have. The telemetry to run it exists in the WMS task stream, the LMS actuals and the PLC logs.

The estate underneath: systems this work touches

Every service on this page reads an estate that already exists. The WMS remains the system of record for inventory and work; the LMS holds the standards; the automation vendors hold their own telemetry; the parcel stack holds the manifest and the invoices. Dataq Software reads first, builds the decision layer beside these systems, and never proposes replacing the WMS that runs the building. Where a layer is missing — no LMS, no dock scheduling system — the baseline prices the gap before anyone proposes a service the estate cannot feed.

Reference architecture: the fulfillment decision stack

The stack follows the same rule as every engagement we run: the WMS stays the system of record, the decision layer reads it, prices the gaps, and returns recommendations through the tools the floor already uses — supervisor dashboards, calibration files for the LMS, disposition rules for returns, staffing curves for the peak plan. Each service is gated by a two-week baseline against your own throughput before anyone commits to build, and each carries an explicit stop condition. Nothing in the stack writes to the WMS without your interfaces and your approval.

Questions warehouse and fulfillment leaders ask us

  • Do we need a new WMS first?

    Almost never. The decision layer reads the WMS you have — Manhattan, Blue Yonder, Körber or warehouse-native — and the two-week baseline tells you whether any WMS change would pay for itself. Replacing a WMS to fix a slotting or wave problem is usually the most expensive possible answer.

  • Our LMS numbers are disputed on the floor. Can you help?

    Yes, and that is the calibration problem. We re-derive actual time distributions per function from task and scan data and show where standards over- or under-state work. Both sides inspect the same evidence, and the standard moves once, on data.

  • Can you work with our automation vendors' systems?

    We read their telemetry through APIs and PLC logs and never re-program their controllers. Robot fleets, AS/RS grids and sorters stay under vendor control; the flow model we build sits beside them and says where the real chokepoint is.

  • Is this another labor-productivity surveillance tool?

    No. We build measurement and decision services; the incentive rules stay yours, the workforce sees the same evidence management sees, and where standards are wrong we say so before the floor has to prove it.

  • Peak is in twelve weeks. Is there time?

    For the simulation and the dated chokepoint list, usually yes — it runs on last season's orders and this year's plan. For a re-slot or a wave-template redesign, we will tell you honestly which changes are safe that close to peak.

  • We are a 3PL with dozens of clients. Does this scale?

    The layer is multi-client by design: client-level order profiles, separate cutoff SLAs, per-client billing evidence for value-added services and detention. The two-week baseline prices it for your busiest building first.

Bring one week of orders, one week of LMS actuals and your worst returns category to a two-week fulfillment baseline: we measure travel time, station idle minutes, standard variance and cut-off attainment against your own data, and price the gap in hours and dollars. You keep the baseline either way. If the numbers support a decision layer, Dataq Software builds it read-first beside your WMS — slotting, wave pressure, standards, returns economics, peak simulation. If the constraint is steel, layout or a rate contract, we tell you that and stop.
