Industrial Equipment & Machinery

Industrial equipment and machinery businesses build twice: once when the machine leaves the factory, and once for every year of the service life that follows. The factory side runs engineer-to-order and configure-to-order through CPQ, engineering, project milestones and long-lead procurement; the installed-base side runs connected-machine telemetry, service parts, field dispatch, uptime guarantees and warranty adjudication across decades and dealer networks. The margin increasingly lives on the second build — service attach, contract profitability, fleet uptime — and the systems that hold it were designed for transactions, not decisions. This page is the engineering picture of both builds: the systems they run on, the points where they leak money, and the six deep builds Dataq Software ships into them.

How the business actually runs

An industrial equipment business builds twice: once at the factory gate and once across the service life that follows. On the build side, a quotation is an engineering act: a sales engineer or a configurator assembles a variant from a complex BOM, checks long-lead components, prices the exception, and commits a delivery date the project plan then has to honor. Engineer-to-order jobs run through engineering release, procurement of items nobody stocks, fabrication, assembly and site acceptance, with earned-value milestones reporting progress to the customer. Configure-to-order lines repeat the pattern at tempo, and every engineering change races the build it touches.

On the service side, the installed base is the real factory: hundreds or thousands of machines in the field, each with a configuration history, a warranty clock, a service contract and a dealer who may or may not share its telemetry. Connected machines stream health data through the fleet platform; disconnected ones report only when they fail. Spares planners forecast demand against failure modes and contract terms; field service dispatches technicians against skills, parts availability and SLA windows; warranty teams adjudicate claims against contract language and machine history. When a failure mode repeats across the fleet, the finding should reach engineering — and usually dies in a spreadsheet instead.

Around both sides runs the compliance perimeter: EU machinery obligations and the connected-product security duties arriving with the Cyber Resilience Act, machine safety standards where workers share space with the equipment, data-protection duties on operator data, and documentation duties that now extend across the machine's whole life. Retrofit and upgrade campaigns cut across the same perimeter, because a connected machine is a product decision as much as a service decision. The data for all of it exists in the estate; the decisions wired to it mostly do not — and that gap is where the programs on this page live.

Where the margin leaks

Ask the service and sales leaders where the money leaks and the answers converge on the same places, described in their own words. Dataq Software scores each leak in the estate before recommending a build, because the dominant leak in one fleet is often minor at the next — and the scoring produces the baseline the program is measured against.

  • Installed base disconnected

    The machines that carry the service business lack telemetry, or their telemetry never leaves a vendor portal nobody integrates. Service stays reactive, parts planning forecasts on history alone, and the reliability knowledge that would drive design improvements accumulates nowhere. Connecting the fleet — and governing the firmware that connectivity brings — is the first engineering conversation in most equipment programs we enter.

  • Dispatch that cannot co-plan skills and parts

    Field service scheduling by phone and spreadsheet books the technician who answers, not the one with the skill, the part and the travel window. First-time fix suffers, SLA breaches follow, and every second visit doubles the cost of a repair that was schedulable. Dispatch orchestration that plans skills, parts and SLA windows together is the difference between a service network and a service cost center.

  • Spares economics nobody can state

    Parts planning runs on moving averages, so stock concentrates on the parts that failed last year and misses the failure modes that are emerging. Obsolescence lands as an emergency; contract fill-rate promises are met by luck. Spares optimization against failure-mode demand, contract terms and fleet population turns the service parts business from inventory folklore into an engineered supply.

  • Warranty adjudicated without evidence

    Claims arrive through dealers and get adjudicated against contract language by people who cannot see the machine history. Fraudulent and borderline claims settle, genuine failure patterns stay invisible, and the analytics that would route design improvements back to engineering never run. Claims joined to machine history and telemetry change both the adjudication and the design loop.

  • Service economics opaque

    Cost per machine, margin per contract and profitability per dealer are unknown because service, parts and warranty data never meet. Pricing is tradition-based; contracts are priced on confidence, not cost. A service economics engine that computes cost and margin per machine, contract and dealer is the prerequisite for outcome pricing — and outcome pricing is where the sector's revenue growth is actually happening.

  • Quotes that promise unbuildable machines

    Configurator rules drift from engineering reality, so quotes commit variants the factory discovers at assembly. Expedite engineering follows, launch dates slip, and the margin leaks quietly through change orders. Configurator engineering with contract tests against the BOM keeps the promise chain honest from quote to delivery.

  • Reliability knowledge trapped

    Failure data accumulates in warranty records, service reports and telemetry, but never becomes features. The design loop closes by anecdote, so the same weaknesses ship in the next generation. Reliability models on the installed base — per failure mode, per configuration, per duty cycle — are what convert field history into engineering decisions and service pricing.

  • Product security arriving late

    Connected machinery inherits product-security duties — vulnerability handling, SBOMs, signed firmware, update guarantees — and most firmware estates were never managed. Dealers install unsigned packages; exposure accumulates across the fleet. The product security program has to run as engineering, against every machine generation, or the compliance duty becomes a recall-shaped problem.

The pattern across all eight leaks: the installed base generates the evidence every decision needs — machine history, telemetry, claims, contract terms — but the evidence lives in silos owned by service, finance and engineering separately. Joining it is a data engineering program first; the pricing, dispatch and reliability models are the easy half of the work.

The builds: six deep engineering services for equipment makers

The catalog below is the spine of our industrial equipment practice. Each build is named for the workflow it changes, sized in the diagnostic, and shipped with the controls the market-surveillance authorities, the insurers and the dealers will expect. None of them require replacing the ERP; all of them require taking the product data contracts seriously.

1. CPQ and configurator engineering with contract tests

The configurator build makes the quote-to-delivery chain honest. Rules are extracted from the current system and engineering's actual practice, then re-encoded as a validated ruleset with contract tests against the BOM: every configuration the configurator can sell is checked for assembly feasibility, long-lead components and routing cost before it reaches a customer. Pricing pulls cost signals from the project history, so exceptions are priced on evidence rather than on the sales engineer's nerve.

The integration keeps the promise chain intact: accepted configurations flow to engineering release and ERP as structured data, engineering changes propagate back into the rules with impact analysis, and every quote carries its rule version so a dispute months later resolves against what the system actually promised. The measured outcomes are quote cycle time, configuration escapes at assembly, and change-order volume traceable to quote errors — the last number usually funds the build.

2. The connected-machine fleet platform

The fleet build gives every machine an identity, a provisioning path and a governed telemetry channel: device identity per unit, secure onboarding when the machine registers, health and usage data flowing into the same lakehouse the factory data uses, and OTA update channels with signing, staged rollout and rollback. Provisioning reaches back into the installed base through retrofit campaigns, because the fleet's value scales with coverage, not with this year's production.

Governance is part of the platform, not a policy document: firmware estates inventoried per machine, SBOMs generated per release, update guarantees tracked against the product-security duties arriving with connectivity, and dealer-installed software brought inside the signing discipline. The measured outcomes are fleet coverage, telemetry quality per machine family, and OTA success rates — the numbers that decide whether the service business can be run on data at all.

3. Field service dispatch orchestration

The dispatch build plans skills, parts and SLA windows together instead of sequentially. Work orders arrive with machine context from the fleet platform — symptoms, telemetry precursors, configuration, history — and the optimizer matches them against technician skills, certifications, parts availability and travel reality, proposing schedules that meet the SLA windows the contracts actually promise. Technicians accept or adjust in the mobile flow; every decision is logged with its inputs.

Where telemetry exists, the build moves from reactive to predicted: health signals raise work orders before the failure, and dispatch folds them into the same optimization as the break-fix queue. First-time fix is the metric that runs the program — because every second visit doubles cost, breaches SLAs and erodes the dealer relationship. The measured outcomes are first-time fix rate, SLA compliance, travel time share and mean time to restore on the connected part of the fleet.

4. Service parts planning and spares optimization

The spares build replaces moving averages with failure-mode demand: forecasts computed from the fleet's population by configuration and duty cycle, from reliability models where they exist, and from contract terms that promise fill rates. Stocking decisions weigh service level against carrying cost per location, with substitution paths and obsolescence handling engineered rather than improvised. Long-tail parts — the ones that make or break old-machine support — get explicit policies instead of folklore.

The integration closes the loop with dispatch: what the technician can actually get decides what the optimizer proposes, and every consumed part updates both the forecast and the failure statistics. Dealer stocking enters the same model where dealer agreements allow the data. The measured outcomes are fill rate on contracted categories, inventory turns on the long tail, and emergency-order share — the last one being where the hidden margin has been hiding.

5. Reliability and warranty analytics on the installed base

The reliability build joins what design and pricing both need: claims normalized into failure-mode taxonomies, service records, telemetry and machine configuration history joined into fleet-level cohorts per model year, configuration and duty cycle. Failure rates become computable per failure mode instead of anecdotal per dealer, and the analytics route repeating patterns to engineering with the evidence attached — the design loop that anecdote was never going to close.

Warranty adjudication runs on the same spine: claims checked against contract language, machine history and telemetry evidence, with the borderline cases routed to reviewers who see the whole picture. Cost per machine and per failure mode becomes a real number, feeding both the service pricing engine and the next product generation's reliability targets. The measured outcomes are warranty cost per unit, repeat-failure share, and the fraction of field findings that reach engineering with evidence within a quarter.

6. Service economics and outcome pricing

The economics build computes what outcome pricing needs: cost per machine, per contract and per dealer from the joined service, parts and warranty data; margin per contract visible to the people who renew it; and the attach economics that decide where connectivity pays. Uptime guarantees get priced against reliability models instead of confidence, and service contracts get designed with the levers — coverage, response windows, parts commitments — priced from evidence.

The published record shows the ceiling here: a construction-equipment manufacturer that connected machines to connected margin and tripled service revenue attach. The build ships with its evidence layer, so contract negotiations and market-surveillance questions are answered from system output rather than from a spreadsheet festival. The measured outcomes are contract margin, attach rate, uptime-guarantee profitability and renewal rate — the numbers the board actually asks for.

The data plane: from dealer paperwork to governed fleet truth

Every decision gap in an equipment business traces to a data gap, and most trace to the seam between the factory systems and the field. Machines register through dealers with varying data quality; service records accumulate in the FSM system; claims arrive as PDFs; telemetry sits in a vendor portal nobody integrated. The data plane build joins the seam: machine identity established once, configuration history and service history joined to it, telemetry landed through the fleet platform, and every feed carrying a contract with an owner and a freshness SLA.

The machine master is the plane's hardest deliverable: one identity per unit across its lifetime — serial numbers, registrations, configurations, retrofits, ownership changes — because every downstream decision (dispatch, spares, warranty, pricing) is made about a machine that has to resolve to one record. Governance is engineered: reconciliation checks against source totals, dealer-data quality scored and coached rather than silently trusted, and schema changes failing loudly at the contract boundary instead of silently inside a forecast.

  • Machine identity and configuration master

    one record per unit across its lifetime — registrations, configurations, retrofits, ownership — so every decision lands on the right machine

  • Telemetry with provisioning status

    health and usage data joined to coverage metadata, so forecasts and reliability models know what they can and cannot see

  • Contract-enforced freshness

    per-feed SLAs with breach alerts to named owners, so a late dealer upload pages someone instead of skewing a parts forecast

  • Dealer data quality scoring

    incoming records scored against reference rules, with coaching loops for the dealers whose data the service business depends on

The decision layer: dispatches, denials and their receipts

Service decisions have short clocks and contractual tails: a dispatch against an SLA window, a parts allocation, a claim denial, a contract price. The decision layer treats each as a governed object. Every decision records its inputs, model versions, thresholds and outcomes in a replay store, so the question of why this technician got this job, or why this claim was denied, is a query. Threshold and policy changes go through review with the challenger's statistics attached, because a denial that cannot explain itself becomes a dispute.

Every model sits on a physics floor: reliability models must respect failure modes and duty cycles, dispatch must respect travel and certification reality, spares forecasts must respect the fleet population that can actually fail. When model and physics disagree, the disagreement is surfaced to the service engineers rather than averaged away. Champion and challenger evaluation runs on shadow schedules and shadow adjudications with the service leaders reading the results — because adoption in a service network is earned at the job site.

The agentic layer: preparation without authority

The backlog work in an equipment business — claim file assembly, quote exception packs, retrofit campaign planning, service report drafting, contract renewal packs — follows patterns an agentic workforce can absorb without touching authority. Dataq Software's agent platform runs digital workers with explicit role scopes: the systems they may read, the documents they may draft, the actions that require a human checkpoint and the budget of work per run. Every run logs its steps and outputs to the same evidence store the human workflow uses.

Agents never dispatch technicians, price contracts or adjudicate claims; they move work to the decision point and stop. A drafted claim file waits for the adjudicator's signature, with every factual claim traced to machine history and telemetry; a quote exception pack waits for the sales engineer; a renewal analysis waits for the account owner. The capacity story is measured in hours returned per service manager per week — the number that shows up in claim cycle time and renewal season.

Integration engineering: the dealers stay, the contracts change

Equipment estates are layered over decades and distributed across a dealer network no vendor diagram ever captures. Our integration posture is contractual: schema contracts with each system owner, data landed through the fleet platform, CDC and governed APIs, write-back through the FSM, ERP and dealer interfaces your change process already controls, and every new component independently deployable behind flags. The ERP is not replaced; it is supplied with better context and drained of the service economics it was never good at.

The dealer edge gets specific engineering, because that is where the fleet fragments. Dealer systems integrate through graded interfaces — full telemetry where agreements allow, structured service records where they do not — with data-quality scoring and coaching loops instead of silent trust. Where a legacy machine cannot be connected, the retrofit pattern applies: provisioning campaigns planned against service windows, with the campaign economics computed before the first machine is touched. This is unglamorous work, and it is why the service builds hold up across a real network instead of a pilot region.

  • Write-back through your interfaces

    dispatches, work orders and claim outcomes flow through the FSM and ERP interfaces your change process already governs, never around them

  • Graded dealer integration

    telemetry, service records and parts sales integrated per agreement tier, with quality scoring instead of silent trust

  • Retrofit campaign pattern

    connectivity rolled onto the installed base against computed economics, planned into service windows, documented as-built

  • Feature-flagged, independently deployable

    every build ships behind flags with rehearsed rollback, because the service network will ask

How an engagement runs

The engagement shape is deliberately short on ceremony and long on measurement. It starts with a two-week diagnostic: one workflow baselined end to end — usually a dispatch lane, a spares category or a warranty family — the leak quantified in the estate's own data, the contracts inventoried, and a written feasibility read the service director and the product organization can both circulate, including the uncomfortable findings. If the delta is real, the build is scoped against the gates; if not, the baseline stays with you.

  • Weeks 1-2: diagnostic and baseline

    one workflow instrumented, leak quantified, contract inventory across factory and field systems, feasibility read with the delta estimate and the confounders named

  • Weeks 3-8: first vertical slice

    one dispatch lane or one spares category live behind flags with replay and evidence running from day one — machine master joined before optimization starts

  • Weeks 9-16: gates, controls, expansion

    model documentation landed, evidence layer live, remaining regions or categories added, capacity telemetry reporting to the service review

  • Quarterly: challenger reviews

    dispatch, spares and pricing challengers evaluated, reliability models retrained on closed work orders, baseline re-verified against service records

Delivery runs in your rhythm: our engineers work in your repositories, ticketing and access model, with the same CI and security review your teams run. Knowledge transfer is a gate condition, not a closing slide — by the expansion phase your engineers ship changes to the builds, because the runbooks and evaluation suites are theirs from week one. Expansion usually follows the service revenue: regions where the fleet is connected first, categories where the economics are proven first.

The team and the operating rhythm

An equipment build team is small and senior: a lead engineer who has run decision systems across factory and field estates, data engineers who own the machine master and the contract layer, ML engineers who own reliability, dispatch and forecasting, a security engineer who owns the product-security posture, and a domain lead who has lived service networks — dispatch desks, spares economics and dealer negotiations. The team works to your release calendar and your dealer-agreement vocabulary from the first week.

The rhythm is the same at every client: weekly service review on the workflow's own telemetry, gate submissions through your change process, quarterly challenger reviews, and an incident practice that treats the machine master and evidence layer as production-critical — because by the second quarter they are. Reviews are scheduled against the service week — dispatch peaks, renewal seasons — and escalations go to named engineers, not to a queue.

Failure modes we design against

Programs like these fail in known ways, and most failure modes are organizational rather than mathematical. We name them in the feasibility read and design against them explicitly, because a program that cannot survive its second quarter is not worth starting.

  • Pilot purgatory

    a demo built on extracted data that never integrates — we ship the first vertical slice through your change process, flags and gates included, within the first two months

  • Fleet coverage mirage

    dashboards built on the connected minority presented as fleet truth — coverage metadata travels with every model, and blind spots are named, not averaged

  • Dealer data decay

    service records trusted silently while quality decays — scoring and coaching loops are part of the build, and the scores report to the service review

  • Dispatch optimization theater

    schedules that win on paper while technicians improvise in the field — travel, certification and parts constraints are encoded as physics, not assumptions

  • Product-security backlog

    connectivity shipped ahead of SBOM and signing discipline — the security build runs beside the fleet platform, and the diagnostic sequences them together

  • Evidence debt

    compliance preparation consuming a team every quarter because artifacts were manual — the evidence layer generates them continuously or the build is not done

Questions equipment leaders ask us

  • Do we need a new telematics platform first?

    No. The builds consume streams from whatever fleet platform exists and establish the machine master above it. Where the platform blocks — some lock telemetry in — the diagnostic prices the integration instead of pretending around it.

  • Will this work with our dealer network's realities?

    That is a design input, not an obstacle. Dealer integration is graded per agreement tier, data quality is scored and coached rather than assumed, and the program economics are computed against the network you actually have.

  • How does this meet the Cyber Resilience Act duties?

    SBOMs per firmware release, signed update channels, vulnerability SLA tracking and support-period evidence ship with the fleet platform. The evidence layer generates what market surveillance will ask for as a by-product of operations.

  • Can we really price uptime guarantees from data?

    Yes, where reliability models exist, and honestly where they do not. The reliability build computes failure rates per configuration and duty cycle; guarantees get priced against those models, and the confidence intervals travel with the price.

  • What happens to our service planners and adjudicators?

    Preparation moves to agents; judgment stays with people, with better context. Programs are justified in first-time fix, claim cycle time and hours returned per manager — numbers the teams verify in the service review.

  • Our machines are old. Does that block it?

    Old machines are the business case. Retrofit campaigns plan connectivity against computed economics and service windows; the parts and reliability work starts on the population you can see and expands as coverage grows.

  • How do you measure success without gaming?

    Baselines are locked during the diagnostic, holdouts are used where experiments are cheap, and every metric reports from system telemetry. The feasibility read names the confounders before anyone commits to a target.

  • Who owns the build after you leave?

    Your teams, with runbooks, contracts, evaluation suites and registry entries transferred as gate conditions during expansion. Several programs run entirely in-house after the second quarter; we stay on call only where asked.

Bring one service workflow — dispatch, spares, warranty or a connected-machine rollout — and we will baseline it in two weeks on your installed-base data. The feasibility read prices the delta in uptime, first-time fix and attach revenue, and it is yours to circulate.
