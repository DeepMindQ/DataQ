Automotive & Discrete Manufacturing

Automotive and discrete manufacturing sells seconds and certainty: the line that runs, the part that never escapes, the launch that lands on time. Every shift the plant takes OEM call-offs over EDI, sequences bodies and subassemblies through stamping, body, paint and final assembly, error-proofs every fastener and connector, and assembles the genealogy a recall might someday ask for. The numbers that decide the margin are known to every operator — OEE on the constraint line, escapes per million, changeover minutes, launch readiness — and the systems that hold them were built for transactions, not decisions. This page is the engineering picture of that plant: the systems it runs on, the exact points where it leaks, and the six deep builds Dataq Software ships into it.

How the operation actually runs

In automotive and discrete manufacturing, the day starts with a schedule nobody chose: the OEM's 830 planning release and 862 shipping schedules arrived overnight through the EDI gateway, were translated into the ERP's demand, exploded through bills of material and routings, and released as production orders to the MES. The MES sequences them against line capacity, tooling and changeover matrices; kanban and line-side replenishment pull components from the supermarket; AGV and AMR fleets ferry what the tugger routes miss. Sequencing matters most where the OEM mandates it: JIT and JIS call-offs tie the plant's output order to the customer's trim line, so a missequence is a claim waiting to happen.

On the line, error-proofing is the constitution. Torque controllers record every fastening curve against the serial; vision stations check presence, orientation and surface; andon holds the line when a station finds what it was built to find. SPC charts watch critical dimensions; capability studies feed the PPAP dossier that opened the part, and engineering changes flow through APQP gates the customer signed. The genealogy record grows with every operation: material lots, fastening curves, vision dispositions, rework loops, ship-to sequences under the 856 ASN the OEM expects to match what it actually received.

The quieter machinery runs underneath: CMMS work orders on presses and robots, die and tooling masters with their maintenance counters, IATF 16949 audit evidence assembled quarterly, warranty claims arriving from dealers and OEMs with 824 responses going back. The paint shop runs its own economy inside the plant: batch queues and oven windows govern color changeovers, so sequencing there decides both the utility bill and the changeover minutes the OEE waterfall records. When a field issue lands, containment starts with paperwork: which lots, which shifts, which fixtures, which torque curves. The answers exist in the estate — scattered across MES extracts, quality PDFs and the ERP — and the days spent assembling them are the leak most quality managers know best.

Where the plant leaks margin

Ask operations leaders where the money leaks and the answers converge on the same places, described in their own words. Dataq Software scores each leak in the estate before recommending a build, because the dominant leak in one plant is often minor at the next — and the scoring produces the baseline the program is measured against.

  • OEE truth nobody trusts

    Availability, performance and quality are assembled by hand from shift reports, so the loss waterfall varies with who compiled it. Micro-stoppages vanish beneath the reporting threshold, changeovers get logged as minutes when they took hours, and improvement programs chase losses that exist mainly in the report. Sensor fabric and loss-accounting telemetry make the waterfall a measurement, and measurements are the only losses a plant can actually recover.

  • Vision stations that stall in pilot

    Defect detection still leans on sample plans and eyes, and where vision exists it detects without deciding: no MES hold, no andon, no disposition trail. Escapes reach the OEM as warranty claims and containment events. The hard parts were never the model — they were lighting, part presentation, rejection hardware and the integration that turns a verdict into a hold the line respects.

  • Sequencing by spreadsheet against JIT/JIS penalties

    Body-shop and trim sequencing obeys OEM-mandated order rules, changeover matrices and paint-batch constraints that live in planners' heads and spreadsheets. When the 862 shifts mid-week, replanning takes hours the schedule does not have, and expedites eat the changeover savings. Constraint-aware sequencing with drum-buffer-rope logic finds hours per week the plant had stopped counting.

  • Containment and 8D by archaeology

    A field issue triggers the same scramble: which lots, which shifts, which fixtures, which fastening curves. Genealogy assembled from MES extracts and paper travelers takes days, and containment either over-scopes into good stock or under-scopes into shipped escapes. Genealogy captured at the source turns recall scoping into a query and the 8D into evidence assembly.

  • Warranty findings that never reach the line

    Claims arrive as dealer and OEM data, get adjudicated by finance, and die in a spreadsheet the plant never sees. The failure modes that repeat across shifts and suppliers stay invisible because warranty codes, process telemetry and torque curves never meet in one query. The loop from field to fixture is the highest-leverage quality loop in this industry, and it is usually broken at the data layer.

  • Reactive maintenance on constraint equipment

    Presses, robots, transfer lines and paint ovens fail on their own schedule because precursors never reach a model. Calendar PMs catch what calendars catch; die counts drift from reality. Vibration, current and cycle telemetry wired into CMMS work orders moves interventions into planned windows — and on the constraint line, every avoided unplanned hour is sales.

  • Traceability built for the audit, not the plant

    IATF 16949 evidence gets assembled quarterly as binders, then decays. The plant needs traceability daily — for holds, rework and PPAP deviations — but the systems record transactions, not traversable history. When evidence is a by-product of running the system, audits shorten and containment accelerates from the same investment.

  • Energy and utility economics invisible

    Compressed air, ovens and paint shops dominate the utility bill, but costs are allocated by ratio, so per-unit energy economics never exist. Submetering tied to production telemetry makes the numbers measurable — and paint-batch sequencing that respects energy states starts to pay twice: on the utility bill and on changeover minutes.

The pattern across all eight leaks: decisions are being made without their context, and context is a data engineering problem before it is a modeling problem. That is why the first build in most automotive programs is the plant data backbone — not a smarter inspection model.

How Dataq Software works automotive and discrete

Dataq Software builds inside the estate you already run. We do not arrive with a replacement MES, a smart-factory platform sale, or a deck that ends where the hard integration begins. The engagement model is engineering-first: a two-week diagnostic that baselines one workflow end to end — a line, a launch, a containment process, a scheduling desk — then a scoped build that ships behind your change process, your OT security review and the IATF audit calendar your quality team already serves.

Three engineering commitments shape everything we deliver here. First, every automated decision carries replay: inputs, model versions, thresholds and outcomes are stored, so the question of why a station rejected this part nine months ago is a query, not an investigation. Second, every model is bound to its physics — vision to optics and part presentation, maintenance to the failure mode's signature, sequencing to the plant's real constraint set — because models that ignore how the line runs get overruled within a shift. Third, every build lands with its evidence layer: the artifacts a customer audit or registrar will ask for are generated continuously by the system.

The published record behind this approach includes a global automotive OEM that gained 7.2 OEE points across 14 plants, from retrofit sensor fabric through scheduling decisions — the delta measured against a baseline locked during the diagnostic, not a projection. That program is the template for the builds below: instrument what pays, close the loop from prediction to work order and hold, and let the evidence layer shorten every audit that follows. Where the metric is clean we contract against the delta; where it is confounded we scope fixed-fee and say so in the feasibility read.

The builds: six deep engineering services for automotive and discrete

The catalog below is the spine of our automotive and discrete practice. Each build is named for the workflow it changes, sized in the diagnostic, and shipped with the controls the customer's specific requirements and the registrar will expect. None of them require replacing the MES; all of them require taking the data contracts seriously.

1. Line sensor fabric and OEE truth

The fabric build instruments the line the plant cannot shut down: clamp-on current and vibration on spindle and servo drives, cycle and micro-stoppage capture from PLC and bus traffic, thermal on ovens and panels, counts and states from the station level. Edge gateways buffer through outages and publish through the OT security model. Points are chosen backward from the loss waterfall — where availability, performance and quality actually bleed — not forward from a sensor catalogue, so every channel installed has a decision it serves.

The OEE service consumes the fabric: availability from states, performance from cycle truth, quality from inspection verdicts, micro-stoppages classified by pattern. The loss waterfall becomes a live measurement with lineage, reviewed in the same weekly meeting where estimates used to argue. In the published OEM program, this fabric — wired through to scheduling decisions — carried 7.2 OEE points across 14 plants, because a loss nobody could see is a loss nobody has ever recovered.

2. Vision inspection closed into holds and dispositions

This build starts at the station: lighting, optics, part presentation and rejection hardware are engineered before any model is trained, because a detector that cannot act is a screensaver. Detection covers surface defects, presence and orientation, assembly verification and marking checks, with per-station golden sets and confusion matrices the quality engineers own. Station logic integrates to the line: reject actuated, MES hold raised, andon notified, disposition stored against the serial with its image.

Retraining runs as a governed loop: false accepts and false rejects are labeled by the quality team, thresholds are tuned to the control plan's escape and scrap trade-off, and drift is monitored per station with golden-set revalidation. The measured outcomes are escape rate, false-reject scrap and station cycle time. A station reaches production when the false-reject complaints stop and the escape audit trail starts answering for itself.

3. Sequencing and changeover optimization for JIT and JIS

The scheduling build encodes what the veterans know: OEM sequence rules from the 830 and 862, changeover matrices, paint-batch and cure constraints, tooling and die availability, drum-buffer-rope logic around the constraint line. Solvers propose sequences and campaigns against measured objectives — schedule adherence, changeover minutes, paint efficiency, energy states — and planners accept, adjust or reject with the trade-offs visible. Every proposal carries its reasons, so trust accumulates on evidence instead of seniority.

When the OEM shifts the release mid-week, the same optimizer replans in minutes, and the agentic layer assembles the recovery options before the morning meeting. Authority stays with the planner; the system moves the preparation. Programs measure schedule adherence, changeover minutes and expedite counts — the numbers that show whether the decision layer has finally caught up with the 862.

4. Genealogy and recall scoping as a query

The genealogy build joins what containment needs: material lots from ERP and the supermarket, serials from the line, fastening curves from torque controllers, vision dispositions, rework loops, ship-to sequences from the 856. The store is traversable in both directions — from serial to supply chain, from suspect lot to what shipped where — and every hop carries its evidence, so the answer to a containment question arrives with the proof attached.

Scoping becomes a query with a turnaround measured in minutes, and the over-containment tax falls accordingly: good stock stops being quarantined because scoping was uncertain. The same store feeds daily work — rework dispositions, PPAP deviation packets, customer quality requests — so the investment pays between recalls, not only during them. This is the build the 8D workbench and the warranty loop below both lean on.

5. Warranty analytics feeding the plants

The warranty build closes the loop from field to fixture: claims data from dealer and OEM feeds normalized into failure-mode taxonomies, joined to the genealogy store so every claim points back to build date, shift, station, supplier lot and fastening evidence. Cohort analyses expose the patterns finance adjudication never sees — failure modes that repeat across plants, suppliers or launch dates — and route them to the 8D owners with the evidence already assembled.

The 8D workbench runs on the same spine: containment scoping from genealogy, root-cause hypotheses with the process telemetry attached, corrective actions tracked to closure, and lessons encoded back into error-proofing and control plans. The measured outcomes are claim cost per unit, repeat-failure share and containment cycle time. Plants stop relearning what the field already taught them — the quietest, largest quality saving this industry offers.

6. Predictive maintenance, tooling and die health

This build moves the maintenance mix on the equipment that stops the line: presses, robots, transfer systems, ovens and the dies themselves. Condition indicators — vibration spectra, current signatures, cycle counts, temperature deltas, die counter readings — are scored against failure-mode models, and interventions land as CMMS work orders with the evidence attached: the trend, the anomaly, the recommended window and the parts.

Planners negotiate windows against the production schedule with real data, and every closed work order feeds back as a label. Die and tooling masters get the same treatment: counts reconciled to telemetry, condition inferred from force and dimensional drift, maintenance triggered by state instead of by calendar. The program metric is the planned-versus-reactive shift — because that shift is what converts into OEE and fewer escapes at the same time.

The data plane: from micro-stoppages to governed streams

Every decision gap on a plant floor traces to a data gap, and most trace to the same habit: controllers talk to the historian, the historian talks to reports, and the reports talk to nobody with authority. The data plane build keeps the systems of record and gives the decision layer its own supply: line telemetry through OPC UA at cycle resolution, CDC from ERP, MES and EDI transactions, governed file intake from torque exports and quality documents — every feed with a contract.

The contracts are engineered, not asserted. Each feed carries an owner, a freshness SLA and a reconciliation check against source totals; asset naming follows ISA-95 so station 40 means the same thing in the historian, the MES and the model. Fastening curves and images land with their calibration references and timestamps, because a torque curve without its tool and program version is a rumor. AGV and AMR telemetry joins the same plane — battery health, route completions, queue times — so line-side logistics failures stop hiding behind production numbers. When a schema changes upstream, the contract fails loudly at the boundary instead of silently inside a hold decision.

  • Cycle-resolution line telemetry

    states, counts and micro-stoppages captured at the source with store-and-forward, so the loss waterfall sees what a shift report rounds away

  • ISA-95 asset naming

    station, line and work-center names resolved once, so OEE, quality and maintenance features join without a mapping spreadsheet

  • Torque and vision evidence capture

    fastening curves and images stored with tool, program and calibration references, attached to the serial they belong to

  • Contract-enforced freshness

    per-feed SLAs with breach alerts to named owners, so a late ERP load pages someone instead of skewing a scheduling feature

The decision layer: holds, thresholds and replay

Line decisions have short clocks and long tails: a reject at a station, a hold on a lot, a maintenance window, a sequence proposal. The decision layer treats each as a governed object. Every decision records its inputs, model versions, thresholds and outcomes in a replay store, so a quality engineer investigating an escape months later retrieves exactly what the system saw and chose — and every threshold change goes through review with the challenger's statistics attached.

Every model sits on a physics floor: vision decisions respect optics and presentation, maintenance scores must beat the failure mode's physical signature, sequencing proposals must respect the plant's real constraint set. When model and physics disagree, the disagreement is surfaced rather than averaged away. Champion and challenger evaluation runs on shadow data with the plant's quality engineers reading the confusion matrices — because adoption on a line is earned at the station.

The quietest failure in a plant decision layer is threshold drift: stations retuned after a launch, a vendor firmware update, or an engineering change, with nobody recording the new baseline. Six months later the replay store defends a decision the station no longer makes. Threshold changes are gated like code changes — versioned, reviewed and diffed against the golden sets — or the evidence layer quietly stops being evidence.

The agentic layer: preparation without authority

The backlog work around a plant — containment scoping, 8D evidence assembly, PPAP deviation packets, shift-handover summaries, plan-recovery options — follows patterns an agentic workforce can absorb without touching the line. Dataq Software's agent platform runs digital workers with explicit role scopes: the systems they may read, the documents they may draft, the actions that require a human checkpoint and the budget of work per run. Every run logs its steps and outputs to the same evidence store the human workflow uses.

Agents never write to PLCs, torque controllers or MES execution; they move work to the decision point and stop. A drafted containment list waits for the quality engineer's sign-off; a proposed sequence waits for the planner; a recovery option set waits for the morning meeting. Where agents draft 8D sections, the citation obligations hold: every factual claim traces to the case file, and the engineer remains author of record. The capacity story is measured in hours returned per engineer per week — the number plant leadership sponsors, because it shows up in containment cycle time and launch reviews.

Integration engineering: the line stays, the contracts change

Automotive estates are layered over decades, and promising greenfield is the fastest way to lose a plant. Our integration posture is contractual: schema contracts with each system owner, data landed through OPC UA, CDC and governed APIs, write-back through the MES, CMMS and ERP interfaces your change process already controls, and every new component independently deployable behind flags. The MES is not replaced; it is supplied with better context and drained of the reporting it was never good at.

The vendor chain gets the same treatment. Torque tool networks, vision vendors and safety systems sit behind abstraction with contract tests, so a tool or camera swap is a configuration change with a runbook rather than a re-station. Where a legacy historian or line controller cannot be touched, the offload pattern applies: the system keeps running while a monitored, read-only path exports its data with a documented schema. This is unglamorous work, and it is why the builds hold up in production.

  • Write-back through your interfaces

    holds, dispositions and work orders flow through the MES, CMMS and ERP interfaces your change board already governs, never around them

  • Tool networks behind contract tests

    torque, vision and scanner integrations abstracted so a swap is configuration and a runbook, not a shutdown

  • Legacy offload, read-only and monitored

    untouchable controllers keep running while monitored feeds export their data with documented schemas and health checks

  • Feature-flagged, independently deployable

    every build ships behind flags with rehearsed rollback, because the site change board will ask

How an engagement runs

The engagement shape is deliberately short on ceremony and long on measurement. It starts with a two-week diagnostic: one workflow baselined end to end — usually the constraint line or the containment process — the leak quantified in the plant's own telemetry, the data contracts inventoried, and a written feasibility read the plant manager and the OEM-facing quality team can both circulate, including the uncomfortable findings. If the delta is real, the build is scoped against the site's gates; if not, the baseline stays with you.

  • Weeks 1-2: diagnostic and baseline

    one workflow instrumented, leak quantified, contract inventory, feasibility read with the delta estimate and the confounders named

  • Weeks 3-8: first vertical slice

    one line or one lane live behind flags with replay and evidence running from day one — typically the constraint line's loss waterfall or a first vision station

  • Weeks 9-16: gates, controls, expansion

    model documentation landed, evidence layer live, remaining stations added, capacity telemetry reporting to the operations review

  • Quarterly: challenger reviews

    threshold and model challengers evaluated, sequencing strategies re-optimized, baseline re-verified against production records

Delivery runs in your rhythm: our engineers work in your repositories, ticketing and access model, with the same CI and security review your teams run. Knowledge transfer is a gate condition, not a closing slide — by the expansion phase your engineers ship changes to the builds, because the runbooks and evaluation suites are theirs from week one. Programs in this sub-industry usually expand along two axes at once: more lines at the launch site, then the same workflow at sister plants whose deltas the shared baseline can now predict.

The team and the operating rhythm

A plant build team is small and senior: a lead engineer who has run decision systems inside automotive plants, controls and data engineers who own the OT boundary and the contract layer, ML engineers who own vision, prediction and optimization, a security engineer who owns the 62443 posture, and a domain lead who has lived launch, containment and JIT sequencing in production estates. Agentic-workflow specialists join for the preparation builds. The team works to your release calendar and your gate vocabulary from the first week.

The rhythm is the same at every site: weekly operations review on the workflow's own telemetry, gate submissions to the site's change process, quarterly challenger reviews, and an incident practice that treats the decision store and the evidence layer as production-critical — because by the second quarter they are. Reviews are scheduled against shift patterns, not consultancy calendars, and escalations go to named engineers, not to a queue.

Failure modes we design against

Programs like these fail in known ways, and most failure modes are organizational rather than mathematical. We name them in the feasibility read and design against them explicitly, because a program that cannot survive its second quarter is not worth starting.

  • Pilot purgatory

    a demo built on extracted data that never integrates — we ship the first vertical slice through your change process, flags and gates included, within the first two months

  • Station-floor trust collapse

    operators overruling a detector nobody explained — verdicts land with reasons and evidence, and the physics floor is honored before the statistics

  • Loss-waterfall drift

    OEE improving on paper while the line stands still — the waterfall runs from telemetry with reconciliation checks, not from shift reports

  • Genealogy gaps at rework

    containment failing exactly where parts loop back — rework loops are first-class genealogy edges, tested like any other path

  • Maintenance theater

    predictions rising while the CMMS queue stays unchanged — work-order conversion is the program metric, not model accuracy

  • Scope sprawl

    five plants before one line works — expansion follows measured deltas site by site, and the diagnostic decides the order, not the org chart

Questions plant and quality leaders ask us

  • Do we need a new MES first?

    No. The builds consume streams from your MES and write back through its interfaces. Where a legacy data model truly blocks, the diagnostic says so and prices the workaround instead of pretending around it.

  • Will any of this touch the control systems?

    No. Decision services stop at the OT boundary; PLC and safety write paths stay with your existing control chain. Advisories, holds and work orders flow through the interfaces your change board governs.

  • How does this survive a customer audit?

    Every build ships with its evidence layer: genealogy queries, decision replay, threshold histories, calibration references. Auditors walk artifacts the system already produces instead of interviewing whoever assembled a binder.

  • Our plants run different MES vendors. Does that block it?

    It is the normal case. The contract layer normalizes feeds per site while ISA-95 naming keeps asset semantics consistent. Programs start where the delta is largest and expand on measured results.

  • What happens to inspectors and planners?

    Preparation moves to agents; judgment stays with people, with better context. Programs are justified in hours returned per engineer and minutes saved per containment — numbers the teams verify in the operations review.

  • Can this run under our OT security constraints?

    That is a design input, not an obstacle. Ingress publishes through the zones and conduits your 62443 program defines; where that program is incomplete, the security build usually runs first.

  • How do you measure success without gaming?

    Baselines are locked during the diagnostic, holdouts are used where experiments are cheap, and every metric reports from system telemetry. The feasibility read names the confounders before anyone commits to a target.

  • Who owns the build after you leave?

    Your teams, with runbooks, contracts, evaluation suites and registry entries transferred as gate conditions during expansion. Several programs run entirely in-house after the second quarter; we stay on call only where asked.

Pick the workflow that decides your quarter — a constraint line, a launch, a containment process — and we will baseline it in two weeks against your own telemetry. If the delta is real we scope the build; if it is not, you keep the measurement design and the honest read.
