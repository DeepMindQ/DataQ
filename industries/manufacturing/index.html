Manufacturing & Industrial

Manufacturing margin is decided in a stack most visitors never see: sensors and PLCs at the line, SCADA and HMI above them, MES and historians at ISA-95 level three, ERP and planning at level four. Every day this stack runs orders into sequences, materials into genealogies, process telemetry into quality decisions and equipment signals into maintenance windows — while IATF audits, IEC 62443 assessments, energy and carbon reporting draw the boundaries it must stay inside. The plants that compound advantage run governed telemetry into physics-informed models into closed loops, and their operators trust the loop because it predicts, explains and hands off cleanly when it is wrong. This page is the engineering picture of that stack: the systems it runs on, the exact points where it leaks money, and the six deep builds Dataq Software ships across the sector's sub-industries.

How the sector's operations actually run

Follow one production order through the stack and the sector reveals itself. Demand lands in ERP as orders or forecasts; the planning layer explodes the bill of material and routing, checks material availability and finite capacity, and releases production orders to level three. The MES converts each order into operations, dispatches them to line resources, and opens a genealogy record the moment material is issued to work. Operators see the job on HMI panels fed by SCADA; PLCs run the interlocks, drives and error-proofing devices. Quality samples parts into LIMS, capability studies accumulate in the QMS, and the lot cannot ship until release rules pass.

On the process side the physics takes over. A DCS holds the basic control loops; APC layers manage multivariable trade-offs on columns, reactors and dryers; the historian collects thousands of tags at second-level cadence. Batch plants run recipes under ISA-88 with phase logic, unit procedures and electronic batch records; continuous plants run mass balances and yield accounting against the same historian. Shift handover follows a structured log; alarms follow the rationalization ISA-18.2 set in motion years ago and drifting ever since. The IT/OT boundary is a designed interface under IEC 62443, not a firewall nobody owns — at least where the program has reached maturity.

Underneath everything runs the quieter estate: CMMS work orders and calibration records, management-of-change dossiers, energy submeters, emissions stacks, safety-instrumented-system test evidence. Corporate functions pull plant data for ESG reporting; auditors pull quality evidence; customers pull traceability; insurers pull safety programs. Most of these pulls are still manual, because the data exists but never reaches a governed plane. That is the sector's characteristic gap: not a shortage of sensors, but a shortage of decisions wired to the telemetry the plant already produces — and it is the gap every Dataq Software program here is built to close.

Where the money leaks

Ask plant managers where the money leaks and the answers converge on the same places, described in their own words. Dataq Software scores each leak in your estate before recommending a build, because the leak that dominates one site is often minor at the next — and because the scoring itself produces the baseline the program will be measured against.

  • Brownfield lines nobody can instrument

    The assets that matter most run uninstrumented because gut-and-replace is unthinkable and downtime for retrofit is unaffordable. OEE components become estimates, energy per unit becomes a division of invoices by production volume, and the models that twins and predictors need starve for input. Retrofit sensor fabric — non-invasive, phased, installed under planned windows — is the first engineering conversation in most plants we enter.

  • Quality escapes decided by sampling and eyes

    Inspection that depends on sample plans and visual judgment lets defect rates drift with lighting, fatigue and turnover. Escapes reach customers, containment follows, and the scrap and rework costs hide inside standard costs where nobody prices them. Vision systems stall in pilot when lighting, thresholds, part presentation and MES holds were never engineered together — the model was never the hard part.

  • Changeover and waiting losses accepted as weather

    Changeover minutes, starved stations and blocked queues get booked into the OEE waterfall and accepted as structural. The sequencing knowledge that would reduce them sits with two veterans and a spreadsheet. Constraint-aware scheduling with drum-buffer-rope logic and changeover-aware objectives routinely finds hours per week the plant had stopped counting.

  • Traceability assembled by paperwork

    When a customer complaint or a field campaign arrives, scoping affected lots means walking the genealogy through MES extracts, paper travelers and ERP batches. Days pass before containment is certain. Genealogy captured at the source — material issue, process parameters, serials, rework loops — turns recall scoping into a query and an 8D into evidence instead of archaeology.

  • Reactive maintenance on critical assets

    Compressors, presses, robots and boilers fail on their own schedule because failure precursors never reach a model. Calendar PMs catch what the calendar catches. Predictive maintenance wired into CMMS work orders — vibration, thermal, current signatures, cycle counts — moves interventions into planned windows and converts breakdown history into features instead of anecdotes.

  • Energy per unit that nobody can state

    Steam, compressed air and power costs get allocated by ratio, so the product-level energy economics that would redirect investment never exist. Submetering with mass-balance reconciliation, tied to production telemetry, makes energy per unit a measured number with a lineage — and makes the optimization targets real rather than guessed.

  • Planning by spreadsheet under volatility

    Demand shifts, material shortages and campaign changes arrive faster than weekly planning cycles. Planners fight them in spreadsheets; the ERP plan is stale by Tuesday. Agentic planning assistants that propose sequences and campaigns for human approval — with the constraints encoded, not remembered — restore decision speed without removing judgment.

  • IT/OT exposure with physical consequences

    Plant networks flatten quietly as sensors, HMIs and remote vendor access multiply. Legacy Windows boxes, unpatched engineering stations and shared credentials make ransomware and sabotage credible threats with production consequences. IEC 62443 zoning, monitored conduits and recovery drills designed for physical consequence are a program, not a project.

The pattern across all eight leaks is the same: decisions are being made without their context, and context is a data engineering problem before it is a modeling problem. That is why the first build in most Dataq Software manufacturing programs is the plant data backbone, not a smarter algorithm.

How Dataq Software works the manufacturing sector

Dataq Software builds inside the estate you already run. We do not arrive with a replacement MES, a greenfield smart-factory story, or a deck that ends where the hard integration begins. The engagement model is engineering-first: a two-week diagnostic that baselines one workflow end to end — a line, a unit operation, a service network, a planning desk — then a scoped build that ships behind your change process, your OT security review and the access model your plants already trust.

Three engineering commitments shape everything we deliver in this sector. First, every automated decision carries replay: inputs, model versions, thresholds and outcomes are stored, so a quality or audit question two years later is a query, not an investigation. Second, every model is bound to its physics — vision models to optics and part presentation, maintenance models to failure modes, scheduling solvers to the plant's real constraint set — because models that ignore how the plant actually runs get overruled and abandoned. Third, every build lands with its evidence layer: the artifacts an auditor, customer or assessor will ask for are generated continuously by the system, not assembled the week before the visit.

The published record behind this approach: a global automotive OEM that gained 7.2 OEE points across 14 plants, from sensor fabric through scheduling decisions; a specialty chemicals producer that lifted yield 3.4 points while cutting energy per tonne 17% on an APC augmentation program; and a construction-equipment manufacturer that connected machines to connected margin, tripling service revenue attach. Each number came from a locked baseline, not a projection — and each program started smaller than the steering committee expected.

The deep engineering catalog for manufacturing

The catalog below is the spine of our manufacturing practice. Each build is named for the workflow it changes, sized in the diagnostic, and shipped with the controls the site's auditors will expect. The sub-industry pages describe how each build is tuned for automotive lines, electronics fabs, equipment fleets and process units; the engineering underneath stays the same.

1. The plant data backbone — historian to lakehouse, ISA-95 named

The backbone build lands the sector's characteristic gap. Tags, events and transactions arrive through OPC UA and MQTT from SCADA and line systems, through change-data-capture from ERP and MES, through governed file interfaces from LIMS and CMMS — each feed with a schema contract, a named owner and a freshness SLA. Asset naming follows ISA-95 so that a pump, a station and a work center are queryable by role, not by tag folklore. Medallion layers separate raw telemetry, conformed asset history and decision features, and the reconciliation checks against source totals run at ingest rather than at month end.

Genealogy is the backbone's hardest deliverable: material lots, serials, process parameters and rework loops joined into one traversable store that answers containment questions in minutes. Lineage is tracked end to end so a number on a dashboard can be traced to the tag or transaction that produced it. The overnight loads keep running for finance and reconciliation, but decision surfaces stop depending on them — the characteristic inversion that every later build leans on.

2. Retrofit sensor fabric for brownfield assets

Most of the value in a plant lives on assets that cannot be shut down for instrumentation. The fabric build installs non-invasive sensing — clamp-on current and vibration, thermal imaging, acoustic, magnetic flow — on live assets under planned windows, with edge gateways that buffer through network outages and publish through the OT security model, not around it. Points are selected backward from failure modes and energy sinks, not forward from a sensor catalogue, so every channel installed has a decision it serves.

Every signal lands with units, sampling rates, calibration references and health status, because a prediction service fed by drifting sensors is worse than no service. The fabric's own telemetry is monitored — gateway loss, sensor drift, buffer overflow — and its installation is documented as-built so the next program inherits a map instead of a mystery. Typical programs instrument the constraint assets first, where one avoided hour of downtime pays for the phase.

3. Vision inspection and quality analytics closed into line holds

The vision build starts at the station, not the model: lighting, optics, part presentation and rejection hardware are engineered first, because a detector that cannot act is a screensaver. Detection covers surface defects, assembly verification, presence and orientation, and marking checks, with per-station golden sets and confusion matrices reviewed by quality engineers. Station logic integrates to the line: rejects actuated, holds raised in MES, andon notified, and every disposition stored against the serial number with its image.

Retraining follows a governed loop — false accepts and false rejects are labeled, thresholds are tuned to the quality plan's escape and scrap trade-off, and drift is monitored per station with golden-set revalidation. The measured outcomes are escape rate, false-reject scrap and station cycle time. A station reaches production when the false-reject complaints stop and the escape audit trail starts answering for itself; the same telemetry then feeds SPC capability studies and complaint investigations automatically.

4. Predictive maintenance wired into CMMS work orders

Prediction without work orders is a dashboard nobody acts on. The build computes condition indicators from the sensor fabric — vibration spectra, temperature deltas, cycle counts, current signatures — scores them against failure-mode models, and raises work orders into the CMMS through its own integration interface, with the evidence attached: the trend, the anomaly, the recommended window and the parts. Intervention proposals land where planners already work, in the vocabulary the trades already use.

Planners see predicted intervention windows alongside production schedules, so maintenance negotiates with operations over data instead of opinions. Models are validated per failure mode with fleet-level statistics, not per-asset anecdotes, and every closed work order feeds back as a label. The program's metric is a shift in the maintenance mix — planned versus reactive hours — because that shift is what unlocks capacity and reliability at the same time.

5. OT segmentation and the plant cyber program

The security build starts with enumeration: every PLC, DCS node, HMI, engineering station and remote access path listed, owned and risk-ranked. Zones and conduits are designed per IEC 62443 — cell, area and plant levels with controlled crossings — and implemented in phases that respect production calendars. Remote vendor access moves behind brokering with session recording; flat VLANs are cut into zones with monitored conduits; engineering stations get the patching discipline they never had.

Monitoring watches for what matters in a plant: unauthorized PLC writes, engineering-station anomalies, new devices on control VLANs. Recovery is rehearsed with physical consequence in the room — lines restored from known-good images, recipes and parameters verified against the golden copies — because in manufacturing the incident is not over when the server reboots. The evidence layer produces the posture reports an assessor, customer or insurer will ask for as a by-product of running the program.

6. Constraint-aware scheduling and planning assistants

The scheduling build encodes what the veterans know: changeover matrices, tooling and die availability, cure and drying windows, drum-buffer-rope constraint logic. Solvers propose sequences and campaigns against measured objectives — throughput, on-time delivery, changeover minutes, energy per unit — and planners accept, adjust or reject with the trade-offs visible. Every proposal carries the reasons it was built, so trust accumulates on evidence instead of seniority.

Agentic assistants extend the same decision layer: they monitor demand signals, material positions and equipment states, flag plan violations as they emerge, and assemble the recovery options before the morning meeting. Authority stays with the planner; the assistant moves the preparation. Programs measure plan adherence, changeover minutes and expedite counts — the numbers that show whether the decision layer has finally caught up with the plant.

Sub-industry programs: what differs on the ground

The catalog is one spine, four disciplines. The same data plane and decision layer land differently depending on what the plant makes, which systems dominate the estate and who audits it. These are the differences our sub-industry programs are tuned for — the workflows, the systems and the regulators that change on the ground.

  • Automotive and discrete lines

    The tempo is set by OEM call-offs: EDI 830 and 862 releases drive JIT and JIS sequencing, line-side kanban replenishment and error-proofed assembly under IATF 16949. Programs concentrate on OEE truth from sensor fabric, vision inspection closed into andon holds, genealogy that scopes containments in minutes, and warranty analytics that feed 8D investigations back to the plants that built the parts. The OEM's customer-specific requirements ride on top of everything.

  • Electronics and semiconductors fabs

    The tempo is set by equipment: SECS/GEM interfaces stream tool traces, APC and FDC watch every process run, R2R control adjusts recipes, and dispatch rules sequence lots past metrology bottlenecks. Programs concentrate on yield analytics that fuse process, metrology and wafer-map defect data into excursion root cause, tool-health prediction on vacuum and motion subsystems, and dispatch optimization beyond heuristic ceilings — with export controls shaping who may touch the process recipes.

  • Industrial equipment fleets

    The business runs twice: once to build the machine, once for decades of service. Programs concentrate on configure-to-order engineering data, connected-machine platforms with OTA governance, field service dispatch against SLA windows, spares optimization on the installed base, and reliability analytics that close warranty findings into design — under EU machinery and product-security duties that arrive with the connectivity itself.

  • Process units and chemicals plants

    The physics sets the pace: DCS and APC hold the loops, the historian records the truth, ISA-88 recipes govern batches, and PSM programs govern change. Programs concentrate on APC augmentation with constraint-aware models, rotating-equipment health on compressors and pumps, batch genealogy for investigations, energy and emissions accounting with lineage, and tank farm and blend scheduling that respects the lab release clock.

The data plane: historian truth to governed streams

Every decision gap in manufacturing traces to a data gap, and most data gaps trace to the same habit: historians collect tags nobody queries, MES transactions post to reports nobody trusts, and ERP holds the plan the plant actually runs from — in batch, overnight. The data plane build keeps those systems of record exactly where they are and gives the decision layer its own governed supply: near-real-time telemetry through OPC UA, CDC from ERP and MES, governed file intake from labs and maintenance, each feed with a contract.

Governance is engineered, not asserted. Every feed carries a schema contract with an owner, a freshness SLA and a reconciliation check against the source system's own totals; asset naming follows ISA-95 so a station means the same thing in the historian, the MES and the model. Units, sampling rates and quality flags are first-class, because a vibration signal without its calibration reference is noise wearing a label. When a schema changes upstream, the contract fails loudly at the boundary instead of silently inside a model.

  • OPC UA and MQTT ingress

    line and unit telemetry buffered at the edge and published through the OT security model with store-and-forward, so an outage delays data instead of losing it

  • ISA-95 asset naming

    station, unit, line and work-center names resolved once, so OEE, energy and quality features join across systems without a mapping spreadsheet

  • Contract-enforced freshness

    per-feed SLAs with breach alerts to named owners, so a late ERP load pages someone instead of skewing a planning feature

  • Mass and count reconciliation

    production counts and mass balances checked against source totals at ingest, so the decision layer inherits numbers the floor already trusts

The decision layer: thresholds, replay and the physics floor

Manufacturing decisions have short clocks and long tails: a reject at a station, a hold on a lot, a maintenance window, a sequence proposal. The decision layer treats each as a governed object. Every decision records its inputs, model versions, thresholds and outcomes in a replay store, so a quality engineer investigating an escape three months later retrieves exactly what the system saw and chose — and every threshold change goes through review with the challenger's statistics attached.

Every model sits on a physics floor: vision decisions respect optics and presentation constraints, maintenance scores must beat the failure mode's physical signature, scheduling proposals must respect the plant's real bottleneck. When a model and the physics disagree, the disagreement is surfaced rather than averaged away. Champion and challenger evaluation runs on shadow data with the plant's own quality team reading the confusion matrices — because adoption in a plant is earned at the station, not awarded in a steering committee.

The agentic layer: preparation without authority

The backlog work in a plant — evidence assembly for 8D investigations, containment scoping, MOC dossier preparation, shift-handover summaries, plan-recovery options — follows patterns an agentic workforce can absorb without touching the controls. Dataq Software's agent platform runs digital workers with explicit role scopes: the systems they may read, the documents they may draft, the actions that require a human checkpoint and the budget of work per run. Every run logs its steps and outputs to the same evidence store the human workflow uses.

Agents never write to PLCs, DCS or MES execution; they move work to the decision point and stop. A drafted investigation waits for the quality engineer's authorship; a proposed sequence waits for the planner; a scoping list waits for the containment owner. The capacity story is measured in hours returned per engineer per week — the number plant leadership sponsors, because it is visible in investigation cycle time and in what the morning meeting actually gets through.

Integration engineering: the estate stays, the contracts change

Manufacturing estates are layered over decades, and promising greenfield is the fastest way to fail a plant program. Our integration posture is contractual: schema contracts with each system owner, data landed through OPC UA, CDC and governed APIs, write-back through the interfaces your change process already controls, and every new component independently deployable behind flags. The MES is not replaced; it is supplied with better context and drained of the reporting work it was never good at.

The legacy pattern gets the same discipline. Where a DCS or historian cannot be touched, the offload pattern applies: the system keeps running, while its data flows to the plane through a monitored, read-only path with a documented schema. Where a vendor gateway must stay, it sits behind contract tests so a replacement is a migration runbook, not a shutdown. This is unglamorous work, and it is why these builds survive contact with production.

  • Write-back through your interfaces

    holds, dispositions and work orders flow through the MES, CMMS and ERP interfaces your change process already governs, never around them

  • Legacy offload, read-only and monitored

    untouchable systems keep running while monitored feeds export their data with documented schemas and health checks

  • Vendor gateways behind contract tests

    equipment and platform integrations abstracted so a swap is configuration and a runbook, not a shutdown

  • Feature-flagged, independently deployable

    every build ships behind flags with rehearsed rollback, because the site change board will ask

How an engagement runs

The engagement shape is deliberately short on ceremony and long on measurement. It starts with a two-week diagnostic: one workflow baselined end to end, the leak quantified in the plant's own telemetry, the data contracts inventoried, and a written feasibility read the plant manager and the steering committee can both circulate — including the uncomfortable findings. If the delta is real, the build is scoped against the site's gates; if it is not, the measurement design and the baseline stay with you either way.

  • Weeks 1-2: diagnostic and baseline

    one workflow instrumented, leak quantified, contract inventory, feasibility read with the delta estimate and the confounders named

  • Weeks 3-8: first vertical slice

    one line or unit live behind flags with replay and evidence running from day one — typically the constraint asset or the worst-leak station

  • Weeks 9-16: gates, controls, expansion

    model documentation landed, evidence layer live, remaining lines added, capacity telemetry reporting to the operations review

  • Quarterly: challenger reviews

    threshold and model challengers evaluated, maintenance and scheduling strategies re-optimized, baseline re-verified against production records

Delivery runs in your delivery rhythm, not a parallel one: our engineers work in your repositories, your ticketing and your access model, with the same CI and the same security review your teams run. Knowledge transfer is a gate condition, not a closing slide — by the expansion phase your engineers are shipping changes to the builds, because the runbooks and evaluation suites are theirs from week one.

The team and the operating rhythm

A manufacturing build team is small and senior: a lead engineer who has run decision systems inside plants, controls and data engineers who own the OT boundary and the contract layer, ML engineers who own vision, prediction and optimization, a security engineer who owns the IEC 62443 posture, and a domain lead who has lived the workflow — line, fab, fleet or unit operation. Agentic-workflow specialists join for the preparation builds. The team works to your release calendar and your gate vocabulary from the first week.

The rhythm is the same at every site: weekly operations review on the workflow's own telemetry, gate submissions to the site's change process, quarterly challenger reviews, and an incident practice that treats the decision store and the evidence layer as production-critical — because by the second quarter they are. Reviews are scheduled against the plant's shifts, not against a consultancy calendar, and escalations go to named engineers, not to a queue.

Failure modes we design against

Programs like these fail in known ways, and most failure modes are organizational rather than mathematical. We name them in the feasibility read and design against them explicitly, because a program that cannot survive its second quarter is not worth starting.

  • Pilot purgatory

    a demo built on extracted data that never integrates — we ship the first vertical slice through your change process, flags and gates included, within the first two months

  • Tag folklore

    asset names that mean different things in the historian, the MES and the model — ISA-95 naming with contract tests is a gate, not an optimization

  • Trust collapse at the station

    operators overruling a model nobody explained — decisions land with reasons attached, and the physics floor is honored before the statistics

  • Maintenance theater

    predictions rising while the CMMS queue stays unchanged — work-order conversion is the program metric, not model accuracy

  • Evidence debt

    audit preparation consuming a team every quarter because artifacts were manual — the evidence layer generates them continuously or the build is not done

  • Scope sprawl

    five plants before one line works — expansion follows measured deltas site by site, and the diagnostic decides the order, not the org chart

Questions plant leaders ask us

  • Do we need a new MES before any of this?

    No. The builds consume streams from your MES and write back through its interfaces. Where the MES truly blocks — some legacy data models do — we scope that honestly in the diagnostic instead of pretending around it.

  • Will the models touch our control systems?

    No. Decision services stop at the OT boundary; write paths to PLCs and DCS stay with your existing control chain and safety reviews. Advisories, holds and work orders flow through the interfaces your change process governs.

  • How does this pass a customer or registrar audit?

    Every build ships with its evidence layer: genealogy queries, decision replay, threshold change history, calibration references. Auditors walk artifacts the system already produces instead of interviewing whoever assembled a binder.

  • Our plants run different systems. Does that block it?

    No — it is the normal case. The contract layer normalizes feeds per site while ISA-95 naming keeps asset semantics consistent. Programs start where the delta is largest and expand on measured results.

  • What happens to the planners and quality engineers?

    Preparation moves to agents; judgment stays with people, with better context. Programs are justified in hours returned per engineer and minutes saved per investigation — numbers the teams themselves verify in the operations review.

  • Can this run with our OT security constraints?

    That is a design input, not an obstacle. Ingress publishes through the zones and conduits your 62443 program defines; where the program is incomplete, the security build usually runs first, and the diagnostic says so.

  • How do you measure success without gaming?

    Baselines are locked during the diagnostic, controls or holdouts are used where experiments are cheap, and every metric reports from system telemetry. The feasibility read names the confounders before anyone commits to a target.

  • Who owns the build after you leave?

    Your teams, with runbooks, contracts, evaluation suites and registry entries transferred as gate conditions during expansion. Several programs run entirely in-house after the second quarter; we stay on call only where asked.

Bring one line, one unit or one fleet and we will baseline it in two weeks, quantify the leak in your own telemetry, and tell you plainly whether the build is worth the change budget. The first conversation costs an hour; the feasibility read is yours to keep.
